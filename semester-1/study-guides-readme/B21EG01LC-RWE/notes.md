## Block 1: Glimpses of Communication

### Unit 1: Introduction to Communication

#### 1.1.1 Communication

**Theory**
Communication (Latin *communicare* = to share) is the process of exchanging information between individuals using a common system of symbols, signs, gestures or behaviour. Aristotle called man "a social animal" — communication is foundational to collective human existence. The basic model features **Sender → Message → Receiver → Feedback**. Feedback is necessary for communication to continue; it becomes the next message in a two-way process. Human communication evolved from gestures and sounds to language, writing (cuneiform, hieroglyphs, papyrus), paper (China), printing, photography, telegraph, fax, radio, television and internet — connecting the world as a global village.

**Important Points**

- **Sender:** person who initiates communication and produces the message
- **Receiver:** processes and understands the message; sends feedback
- **Two-way process:** both parties participate actively; feedback becomes the next message (e.g. coffee shop order dialogue)
- History milestones: **paper** (China), **printing**, photography, telegraph, fax, radio, TV, internet
- Ancient writing: cuneiform, hieroglyphs, papyrus; homing pigeons used for messages (last pigeon-post service in Odisha, India)
- Even a slight facial expression can convey meaning — communication is continuous in human life

**For Exam**
Communication is the exchange of information using shared symbols between sender and receiver with feedback. **Primary purpose:** to exchange information, ideas and feelings between sender and receiver. It is a two-way process requiring active participation. The Latin origin is *communicare* (to share). Key milestones in its history include paper and printing *(also telegraph, radio, TV, internet)*. The basic model: sender produces message, receiver decodes and responds with feedback, continuing the cycle.

#### 1.1.2 Types of Communication

**Theory**
Communication is classified by the **flow of information**. One-way communication sends information from sender to receiver without feedback — reading a book is an example. Two-way (interpersonal) communication involves both parties transmitting information, as in instant messaging or conversation.

**Important Points**

- **One-way:** sender → receiver, no feedback scope (reading a book, some broadcasts)
- **Two-way / Interpersonal:** both sender and receiver transmit information
- **Symmetric two-way:** equal parties — conversation between friends
- **Asymmetric two-way:** one party has more power — teacher lecturing, boss giving orders
- Example — symmetric: two friends chatting equally; asymmetric: employee listens while manager instructs

**For Exam**
Types of communication by flow: one-way (no feedback, e.g. reading) and two-way/interpersonal (both parties involved). Two-way is further divided into symmetric (equal power) and asymmetric (dominant/subordinate relationship). Communication requires feedback in two-way exchanges for effectiveness.

- **1-way** = no feedback (reading a book)
- **2-way** = with feedback → Symmetric / Asymmetric

#### 1.1.2.1 Flow of Information

**Theory**
The **flow of information** determines whether communication allows feedback. One-way flows from sender to receiver only; two-way enables dialogue and correction of misunderstandings.

**Important Points**

- **One-way:** sender → receiver; no feedback scope — reading a book, some announcements
- **Two-way (interpersonal):** both parties transmit — conversation, instant messaging, interview
- **Symmetric:** equal parties — friends conversing on equal terms
- **Asymmetric:** power imbalance — teacher lecturing, manager instructing employee
- Feedback in two-way communication becomes the next message in the cycle
- One-way without feedback: reading a book (you are receiver, author is sender, no direct reply)

#### 1.1.3 Methods of Communication

**Theory**
Methods describe the **channel** through which messages travel. **Verbal communication** uses language in spoken/oral or written forms. **Non-verbal communication** relays messages without words, often enriching verbal messages through visual/symbolic and physical means.

**Important Points**

- **Verbal — Oral:** spoken word — face-to-face, telephone, interviews, speeches, debates, voice notes
- **Verbal — Written:** email, texts, letters, reports, SMS, social media posts, documents, handbooks
- **Non-verbal — Visual/Symbolic:** symbols, drawings, placards, traffic lights, illustrations
- **Non-verbal — Physical:** gestures, body language, facial expressions, tone, posture, touch, gaze, eye contact, proxemics
- Non-verbal often **supplements** verbal communication; a firm handshake conveys cordiality across cultures
- Written communication is generally more formal than oral; organisational communication follows set formats
- **Who relies on language *(Q55)*:** Human beings / Humans

**For Exam**
Methods of communication are verbal and non-verbal. Verbal includes oral (spoken — face-to-face, phone) and written (email, letters, reports). Non-verbal includes visual/symbolic (traffic symbols) and physical (gestures, body language, tone, posture, proxemics). Non-verbal communication frequently enhances verbal messages. **Humans** rely on language for communication.

#### 1.1.4 Major Styles of Communication

**Theory**
Communication styles reflect how a person expresses feelings and handles disagreement. Four major styles appear in the SLM: aggressive, passive, passive-aggressive and assertive. Only assertive style balances self-expression with respect for others and allows healthy feedback.

**Important Points**

- **Aggressive:** raised voice, shouting, domination, threatening, bullying — ineffective; no scope for feedback
- **Passive:** accepts silently despite disagreement — lack of confidence; may cause long-term stress and anger build-up
- **Passive-Aggressive:** appears passive but expresses disagreement subtly — sulking, rumours, silent treatment, back-handed compliments
- **Assertive (most effective):** expresses own needs while considering others; clear, confident; no domination/subordination
- Assertive example: "I understand your point, but I disagree because the deadline is too short."
- Passive example: boss says "work overtime" — employee says "Okay" despite disagreement

**For Exam**
Four communication styles: aggressive (domination, bullying — ineffective), passive (silent acceptance — lack of confidence), passive-aggressive (subtle resentment), and assertive (most effective — clear expression with consideration for others). Assertive communication shows confidence and reduces misunderstanding.

- **P** = Passive-Aggressive (hidden anger)

#### 1.1.5 Encoding and Decoding

**Theory**
Human language is symbolic. **Encoding** is the sender's process of converting thoughts or ideas into symbols or codes before transmission. **Decoding** is the receiver's process of interpreting those codes to extract meaning. Shared language and symbols are essential for successful communication.

**Important Points**

- **Encoding:** sender converts ideas into verbal or graphic symbols (e.g. red roses = love)
- **Decoding:** receiver interprets symbols to understand meaning
- Encoding example: A gives red roses to B → roses encode love; B decodes the symbol
- Decoding failure: tourist asks directions in English; pedestrian does not understand → communication fails
- If receiver cannot decode, communication becomes ineffective regardless of sender's intent
- Symbols include words, gestures, objects and cultural signs understood by both parties

**For Exam**
Encoding is the sender's conversion of thoughts into symbols before transmission; decoding is the receiver's interpretation of those symbols. Example: red roses encode love; the receiver must decode the symbol. If the receiver lacks the shared code (e.g. different language), decoding fails and communication is ineffective.

### Unit 2: Different Forms and Techniques of Communication

**Theory**
Communication forms are classified by **channel**, **style** and **number of participants**. Effective communication skills are essential for professional success. Modern informal communication (social media, texting) sometimes reduces awareness of professional norms, yet strong skills remain critical in the workplace.

**Important Points**

#### 1.2.1.1 Verbal and Non-verbal Channels

**Theory**
**Verbal communication** uses language orally or in writing. **Non-verbal communication** relies on external aspects — body language, symbols, appearance — and is often more spontaneous than words. It is most effective as a supplement to verbal communication.

**Important Points**

- **Oral examples:** face-to-face, telephone, interviews, speeches, meetings, debates, lectures, group discussions
- **Written examples:** letters, minutes, reports, notices, memos, emails, instant messaging
- **Paralanguage/Vocalics:** how something is said — pitch, tone, hesitation sounds — not the words themselves
- **Kinesics:** body movements and gestures as non-verbal communication
- **Proxemics:** role of space (sitting front row = interest in lecture)
- **Chronemics:** role of time (arriving late to interview = bad impression)
- **Haptics:** role of touch (hug when sad = comfort)
- Handshake = effective non-verbal supplement; firm yet warm handshake creates good impression

**For Exam**
Verbal communication is oral or written. Non-verbal includes paralanguage/vocalics (tone, pitch), kinesics (gestures), proxemics (space), chronemics (time) and haptics (touch). Non-verbal communication supplements verbal messages and is often more spontaneous and accurate in expressing mood.

**For Exam**
Techniques for effective communication include developing curiosity, displaying enthusiasm, being empathetic (with mirroring), reflecting honesty, using appropriate humour, maintaining positivity, ensuring clarity and choosing words carefully. Avoid filler words (oh, right, so). Mirroring = subtle imitation of speaker's gestures. **Rhetoric** is the art of effective or persuasive speaking and writing.

### Unit 3: Barriers to and Guidelines for Effective Communication

**Theory**
Effective communication occurs when the message reaches the receiver without loss or change in meaning. **Barriers** prevent this. Identifying and removing barriers improves communication skills in personal and professional contexts.

**Important Points**

- **Physical barriers:** defective equipment, noise, closed doors, distance between sender and receiver
- **Physiological barriers:** biological conditions (dyslexia, nerve disorders, ADHD) affecting composition/reception
- **Psychological barriers:** prejudice, lack of trust, lack of attention, poor retention
- **Linguistic/Semantic barriers:** poor clarity, dialects, jargon, same word different meanings, wrong translation
- **Perceptual barriers:** different perceptions based on education, occupation, age; **stereotypes** = fixed wrong ideas about a group
- **Cultural barriers:** lack of awareness of other cultures; different cultural responses
- **Organisational barriers:** policies, rules, restrictions in institutions
- **Gender barriers:** gender-based communication differences
- **Emotional barriers:** stress, anger, fear affecting message delivery/reception
- **Mechanical/Technological barriers:** outdated machines, equipment failure
- **Lingo:** specialised vocabulary or jargon used by a particular group or profession — informal word for jargon *(PYQ Q3)*
- Jargon / lingo example: *myocardial infarction* vs heart attack — patient may not understand medical term
- Physical example: phone call at noisy railway station — noise interferes with message
- **Physical barriers (list):** noise, distance, poor network, environmental distractions, physical disabilities, faulty equipment, poor lighting, closed doors, time zones, geographical separation
- **Cultural barriers:** different customs, values, beliefs, language differences, non-verbal differences (gestures, eye contact), ethnocentrism, lack of cultural awareness
- **Filter:** avoiding certain topics in formal communication

**For Exam**
Ten barrier types: physical, physiological, psychological, linguistic/semantic, perceptual, cultural, organisational, gender, emotional, and mechanical/technological. **Lingo** = specialised vocabulary/jargon of a group (informal jargon). Jargon creates linguistic barriers. Dialects are language variations. Stress is an emotional barrier. Institutional rules create organisational barriers. Stereotypes are perceptual barriers.

#### 2.1.1 Different Purposes of Reading

**Theory**
Reading is the intellectual process of **decoding** a text and understanding its abstract meaning (ideas, perspectives, emotions). The writer **encodes** abstract ideas into language; the reader decodes using shared language. We read for different purposes using different methods — newspapers for information, articles for ideas, academic books for knowledge, fiction for pleasure, reference books for specific facts.

**Important Points**

#### 2.2.3.2 Close Reading Step 2 — Patterns and Methods

**Theory**
The second pass examines how the text is constructed — organisation, recurring themes, literary devices and word choice. John Ciardi's purpose of close reading is to **explicate** the text — to explain *how* the poem/text means, not just *what* it says (*How Does a Poem Mean?*).

**Important Points**

- How has the text been organised? Chronological? Thematic? Argumentative?
- Recurring themes, keywords or phrases — why might the author repeat them?
- Literary devices: metaphor, simile, irony, symbolism, alliteration, imagery
- Word choice (diction): formal vs informal; connotations of specific words
- Sentence structure: short vs long sentences; effect on pace and emphasis
- **John Ciardi (Q74):** purpose of close reading = to explicate — explain *how* a poem means
- **Lyric poetry:** expresses personal emotions and feelings of the poet; often musical; subjective and emotional
- **Poem vs Poetry:** **Poetry** = the literary art form/genre as a whole; **Poem** = a single individual composition within poetry
- **Imagery:** vivid descriptive language appealing to the senses (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch) that creates mental pictures; common in poetry
- **Simile:** direct comparison using **like** or **as** — *"O my luve is like a red, red rose"* = love compared to a rose (simile)
- **Denotation** = literal/dictionary meaning of a word; **Connotation** = emotional/cultural associations (e.g. *home* = warmth, not just a house)
- **Dramatic monologue:** a poem in which a single speaker addresses a silent listener, revealing character through speech
- **My Last Duchess (Robert Browning):** Duke shows visitor a portrait of his late wife; reveals jealousy, pride and control; implies he ordered her death — classic Victorian dramatic monologue

**For Exam**
Close reading Step 2: examine patterns and methods — organisation, recurring themes, literary devices and diction. John Ciardi: explicate *how* a text means. Lyric poetry = personal emotion. Poetry = genre; poem = one composition. Imagery = sensory mental pictures. Simile uses like/as. Denotation = dictionary meaning; connotation = associations. Dramatic monologue = one speaker, silent listener (e.g. Browning's *My Last Duchess*).

**Theory**

#### 3.1.6 Preposition

#### 3.1.7.1 Types of Conjunctions — Reference

#### 3.2.1 Sentence and Its Parts

**For Exam**

#### 3.2.3 Question Tags

#### 3.2.4 Sentence Structure and Transformation

**Theory**
By structure, sentences are **simple** (one main clause), **compound** (two+ main clauses) or **complex** (main + subordinate clauses). **Transformation** converts one type to another — statements to questions, assertive to exclamatory, etc.

**Important Points**

- **Simple:** one main clause — Birds fly.
- **Compound:** two+ main clauses joined by coordinating conjunction — Birds fly **and** fish swim.
- **Complex:** one main + subordinate clause(s) — Birds fly **when** spring arrives.
- **Positive:** affirmative without negative words
- **Negative:** contains not, never, hardly, scarcely — Hurry up! We have hardly one hour left.
- Assertive → Interrogative: She is a student. → **Is she a student?**
- Assertive → Exclamatory: It is a beautiful day. → **What a beautiful day it is!**
- Imperative → Interrogative: Close the window. → **Will you close the window?**
- Assertive → Imperative (model): Open the doors → **You should open the doors and windows.**
- **Reported speech basics:** Direct: He said, "I **am** tired." → Indirect: He said that he **was** tired.
- Reported speech rules: present→past; will→would; questions become statement order
- Direct: Alex, "My sister hasn't eaten dragon fruit before." → Alex said her sister **hadn't eaten** dragon fruit before.
- **Active/Passive basics:** Active: The teacher **teaches** the lesson. → Passive: The lesson **is taught** by the teacher.
- Active: She **wrote** a letter. → Passive: A letter **was written** by her. Rule: object of active → subject of passive; be + past participle

**For Exam**
Simple sentence = one main clause; compound = two or more main clauses; complex = main + subordinate. Sentence transformation converts between types. Reported speech shifts tenses (present→past). Active voice emphasises doer; passive emphasises action/object using be + past participle.

### Unit 3: Concord

**Theory**
**Concord** means agreement; in grammar it is **subject-verb agreement** — subject and verb must agree in person and number. Three types: grammatical, notional and proximity.

**Important Points**

- **Grammatical concord — Person:** I → am/was (conditional: I wish I **were**); he/she/it → is/was; they/we → are/were
- **Grammatical concord — Number:** singular subject → singular verb; plural subject → plural verb
- **Notional concord:** meaning-based — collective as unit → singular (The council **has**); as individuals → plural (The jury **were** divided)
- **Concord of proximity:** verb agrees with nearest noun — either…or / neither…nor → nearer subject
- **And** connecting subjects → plural (Meenu and Ajayan **are** doctors) unless single idea (Bread and butter **is** my breakfast)
- **There** sentences: verb agrees with subject after *there* — There **are** twelve mangoes
- **Police, cattle, people** → plural verb
- **A pair of spectacles** → singular verb (**is**, not are)
- **One of/each of/every one of** → plural noun + singular verb — Each of the members **has** voted
- **A number of** → plural (A number of people **are** waiting); **The number of** → singular (The number of artists **is** ten)
- **Each/every/either/neither/somebody/nobody** → singular verb
- **Mathematics, Politics, Physics, news** → singular verb — The news **is** good
- **Many a / More than one** → singular noun + singular verb — Many a student **was** absent
- **Subjects of measurement:** Rs. 100 **is** enough; 250 km **is**; Two-thirds of the water **is** dirty
- **Titles of books:** *Sons and Lovers* **is** a novel by D.H. Lawrence
- **Mary along with her son** **goes** to church (first subject singular); **Neither Sarath nor his friends** **have** won (nearer plural)
- **A bunch of keys is** missing (collective as unit); **The committee meets** every week

**For Exam**
Concord = subject-verb agreement (grammatical, notional, proximity). And → plural unless single idea (bread and butter **is**). Either…or/neither…nor → verb with nearer subject. Police/cattle/people → plural. A pair of spectacles **is**. A number of **are**; The number of **is**. Mathematics/news **is**. Each/every/somebody → singular.

### Unit 4: Tenses

**Theory**
**Tense** denotes time of action — present, past or future. Each tense has four forms: Simple, Continuous, Perfect and Perfect Continuous, giving **12 forms** total. Choosing the correct tense depends on whether the action is habitual, ongoing, completed or connected across time.

**Important Points**

- Three tenses × four forms = **12 tense forms**
- **Simple Present:** habitual action, universal truth, permanent state, scheduled future, instructions — time words: always, usually, often, every day
- **Present Continuous:** action now, temporary situations, definite future plan — is/am/are + verb-ing
- **Present Perfect:** has/have + past participle — recent action (no time stated), continuing from past (since/for), completed with present result
- **Present Perfect Continuous:** has/have been + verb-ing — action started in past continuing to now with duration
- **Simple Past:** completed action at definite past time — regular -ed / irregular forms
- **Past Continuous:** was/were + verb-ing — ongoing past action, background action
- **Past Perfect:** had + past participle — action completed before another past action
- **Past Perfect Continuous:** had been + verb-ing — continuous action before past point
- **Simple Future:** will/shall + base verb — prediction, future event
- **Future Continuous:** will be + verb-ing — ongoing future action
- **Future Perfect:** will have + past participle — completed before future point
- **Future Perfect Continuous:** will have been + verb-ing — duration up to future point

**For Exam**
Tense denotes time of action. Twelve forms arise from three tenses (present, past, future) each with simple, continuous, perfect and perfect continuous forms. Universal truths use Simple Present; actions happening now use Present Continuous; recent unspecified past uses Present Perfect.

**Memory Tip**
Each / Everyone / Nobody → **singular verb**

#### 3.4.2 Present Tenses

**Theory**
The four present tense forms describe current, habitual, ongoing and completed-present actions.

**Important Points**

- **Simple Present:** base/s + es — habit, universal truth, permanent state — He **goes** to temple daily; Earth **revolves** around sun
- **Present Continuous:** am/is/are + ing — action now, temporary — She **is reading** now; I **am feeling** energetic (dynamic feel)
- **Present Perfect:** has/have + past participle — recent/no time stated — I **have finished** homework; **have not seen** cousin lately
- **Present Perfect Continuous:** has/have been + ing — duration from past to now — Her son **has been living** there for five years
- ❌ She **is going** daily → ✅ She **goes** daily (habit); ❌ He **has reading** → ✅ He **is reading**
- Since/for with perfect: I **have lived** here **since** 2010 / **for** ten years

**For Exam**
Present tenses: Simple (habit/truth), Continuous (now), Perfect (recent/unspecified), Perfect Continuous (duration to now). Universal truth → Simple Present. Action now → Present Continuous. Since/for used with perfect tenses.

#### 3.4.3 Past Tenses

**Theory**
The four past tense forms describe completed, ongoing, prior-completed and continuous-past actions.

**Important Points**

- **Simple Past:** regular -ed / irregular — definite past time — Vinay **went** to London last year; I **met** her yesterday
- **Past Continuous:** was/were + ing — ongoing past, background — They **were playing** when it rained; When I came in, you **were talking**
- **Past Perfect:** had + past participle — action before another past — He **had left** before I arrived; Bus **had left** when he reached station
- **Past Perfect Continuous:** had been + ing — duration before past point — She **had been working** for two hours before break
- ❌ When I reached, he **already left** → ✅ he **had already left** (Past Perfect)

**Theory**

| Tense | Simple | Continuous | Perfect | Perfect Continuous |

### Unit 2: Writing Abstract

**Theory**

- **Keywords:** indexing terms for search engines and retrieval — essential for academic databases
- **Structured abstract (IMRaD):** Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion
- **Unstructured abstract:** single paragraph covering aim, methodology, results, conclusion
- Must include: research aim, methodology, results, conclusion

**For Exam**
An abstract is a condensed overview (≤300 words) of a research paper or thesis. It is self-contained, includes keywords for indexing, and differs from introduction (detailed background). IMRaD format: Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion. Used so readers can decide whether to read the full paper.

### Unit 5: Editing and Proofreading

**Theory**
**Editing** improves clarity, precision, accuracy and effectiveness of written work. **Proofreading** is the final step, checking surface-level errors. ESL (English as Second Language) users benefit most from systematic editing.

**Important Points**

- **Editing:** rearranges ideas; ensures coherence using **linking devices** (however, therefore, moreover, firstly, in conclusion); checks grammar, spelling, punctuation
- **Proofreading:** final step of editing; checks **surface-level errors** — spelling, punctuation, typos, formatting; ensures clarity and professionalism before submission
- **Benefits of editing *(Q128)*:** improves clarity, precision, accuracy and effectiveness; ensures coherence with linking devices; removes errors; produces better final draft; ESL users benefit most
- **Ten steps of editing *(Q156)*:**
  1. Take a break after drafting
  2. Read the draft objectively
  3. Check overall structure and organisation
  4. Improve clarity and coherence (linking devices)
  5. Check grammar and sentence structure
  6. Check spelling and punctuation
  7. Verify facts and data
  8. Remove unnecessary words/repetition
  9. Read aloud to catch errors
  10. Final proofread for surface-level errors
- **Final draft** has most clarity, precision and accuracy
- **Proofreading tips:** read objectively; take breaks; use grammar book/dictionary; read aloud; check one error type at a time
- **ESL users** benefit most from editing practice
- Editing exercise: ❌ Indias aspiration to **became** → ✅ India's aspiration to **become**; ❌ observed National Youth Day → ✅ observed **as** National Youth Day
- Linking devices example: Social media connects people. **However**, excessive use affects mental health. **Therefore**, students should set limits. **In conclusion**, balanced use is essential.

**For Exam**
Editing improves clarity, precision, accuracy and effectiveness using linking devices for coherence. **Ten steps:** break → read objectively → check structure → clarity/coherence → grammar → spelling/punctuation → verify facts → cut repetition → read aloud → final proofread. Proofreading is the final step checking surface errors. ESL users benefit most. Final draft is most effective.

### Block 1 — Communication

**1. Communication model** = **SMRF**  
→ Sender · Message · Receiver · Feedback

**2. Communication styles** = **APPA**  
→ Aggressive · Passive · Passive-Aggressive · **Assertive** (best ✅)

**3. Encoding & decoding** = **ED**  
→ **E**ncoding = sender makes symbols · **D**ecoding = receiver understands

**4. Non-verbal types** = **H-P-C-K-V**  
→ Haptics (touch) · Proxemics (space) · Chronemics (time) · Kinesics (gestures) · Vocalics (tone)

**5. Formal flows** = **VHD**  
→ Vertical (up–down) · Horizontal (same level) · Diagonal (across)

**6. By number of people** = **I-D-S-P-M**  
→ Intrapersonal (self) · Dyadic (2) · Small group · Public · Mass

**7. Good communication** = **7 Cs**  
→ Clear · Correct · Complete · Concise · Concrete · Courteous · Considerate

**8. Barriers** = 10 types  
→ Physical · Physiological · Psychological · Linguistic · Perceptual · Cultural · Organisational · Gender · Emotional · Mechanical

**9. Informal talk at work** = Grapevine  
→ Fast, no fixed channel (coffee-break talk)

---

### Block 2 — Reading

**1. Two reading categories** = **EI**  
→ Extensive (wide / habit) · Intensive (detailed study)

**2. Fast reading** = Skim vs Scan  
→ Skim = get the **gist** · Scan = **find** one fact · (unsure? skim first)

**3. Close reading steps** = **3R**  
→ Read thoroughly · Recognise patterns · Reread

**4. Interpretation levels** = **LMM** (textbase)  
→ Linguistic (words) · Microstructure (sections) · Macrostructure (themes)

**5. Poem appreciation** = **PTSDF**  
→ Poet · Theme · Summary · Devices · Famous lines

**6. Kalam’s dream thinkers** = **GEALC**  
→ Gandhi · Einstein · Asoka · Lincoln · Caliph Omar  
*(Book: Ignited Minds · Date: 30 Sept 2001)*

**7. The Open Window (Saki)** = **VFW**  
→ Vera · Framton · Window (symbol of trick / escape)

**8. The Blessed Damozel themes** = **LGR**  
→ Love · Grief · Religion *(white lily = resurrection)*

---

### Block 3 — Language / Grammar

**1. Parts of speech** = **NVAA PPCI** (8)  
→ Noun · Verb · Adjective · Adverb · Pronoun · Preposition · Conjunction · Interjection

**2. Sentence types** = **AIIE**  
→ Assertive (.) · Interrogative (?) · Imperative (command) · Exclamatory (!)

**3. Question tags** = **+/- flip**  
→ + statement → − tag · − statement → + tag  
→ I am → **aren't I?** · Let us → **shall we?**

**4. Sentence structure** = **SCC**  
→ Simple (1 clause) · Compound (2+ main) · Complex (main + subordinate)

**5. Concord (agreement)**  
→ *a number of* → plural · *the number of* → singular

**6. Tenses** = **3 × 4** = 12 forms  
→ Present / Past / Future × Simple / Continuous / Perfect / Perfect Continuous

**7. Passive & reported speech**  
→ Passive = **be + V3** · Reported: present → past · will → would

---

### Block 4 — Writing Basics

**1. Elements of writing** = **CFSS**  
→ Content (what) · Form (how/where) · Structure (order) · Style (manner)

**2. Writing process** = **POWER**  
→ **P**lanning · **O**rganizing · **W**riting · **E**diting · **R**evising

**3. Answering questions** = Think → **IBC** → Check  
→ Introduction · Body · Conclusion

---

### Block 5 — Summary, Essays, Reports

**1. Summary vs précis**  
→ Summary = **no** heading (can be spoken)  
→ Précis = **has** title · written only · about **1/3** length

**2. Abstract format** = **IMRaD**  
→ Introduction · Methods · Results · Discussion *(+ keywords · ≤300 words)*

**3. Essay basics**  
→ Father = **Montaigne** · Structure = **IBC** (Intro · Body · Conclusion)

**4. Good essay features** = **TUCDC**  
→ Thesis · Unity · Coherence · Development · Conclusion

**5. Report questions** = **5 Ws + H**  
→ Who · What · When · Where · Why · How

**6. Informal reports** = **LM**  
→ Letter report · Memo report

**7. Formal report parts** = **FMB**  
→ Front matter · Main body · Back matter

**8. Editing vs proofreading**  
→ Edit first (clarity + linking words) · Proofread last (spelling / surface errors)
