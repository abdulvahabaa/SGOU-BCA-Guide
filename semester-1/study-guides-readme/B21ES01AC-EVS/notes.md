### Unit 1: Environmental Segments

#### 1.1.1 Environmental Segments

**Theory**

Environmental studies is a multidisciplinary field examining biotic and abiotic surroundings that influence survival, development and evolution of organisms. Earth is the only planet with life-sustaining land, water and air. Four environmental segments — atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere and biosphere — interact dynamically; change in one segment affects all others. Mahatma Gandhi observed: "There is enough for man's needs but not for his greed" — human greed has brought untold destruction of nature, resulting in repeated disasters like tsunami, Ockhi, floods and landslides.

**Important Points**

- Four segments: **Atmosphere, Hydrosphere, Lithosphere, Biosphere**
- Environment scale ranges from microscopic to global (marine, atmospheric, terrestrial)
- Integrates physical sciences, economics, humanities, social sciences, ethics, law, pollution control
- Man is part of nature, not above it; both are interrelated and interconnected
- Principles from geography, anthropology, politics, sociology guide environmental studies

**For Exam**

Environmental studies is a multidisciplinary academic field dealing with the environment and its functions. The environment includes biotic and abiotic surroundings affecting organism survival and evolution. Four environmental segments — atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere and biosphere — are interlinked and control life on Earth. Any change in one segment affects the nature and functioning of others. Human greed, not need, drives nature's destruction.

#### 1.1.1.1–1.1.1.4 Four Environmental Segments

**Theory**

The four environmental segments form the physical basis of all life. Atmosphere is a 5000-million-ton gas envelope; hydrosphere covers 71% of Earth as liquid, vapour and ice; lithosphere is the rigid outer Earth of crust, soil and upper mantle; biosphere is the realm of living organisms divided into ecosystems with producers, consumers and decomposers.

**Important Points**

- **Atmosphere:** Layer of gases retained by Earth's gravity — N₂ 78%, O₂ 21%, CO₂ 0.33%, Ar 0.93%; also He, CH₄, O₃, Ne, Xe; water vapour varies seasonally; above 50 km only O₂, O₃, He, H
- **Hydrosphere:** Total water on Earth — surface, underground, air — 71% Earth covered; oceans, seas, rivers, lakes, glaciers, groundwater; frozen part = **cryosphere**; Earth called **blue planet**
- **Lithosphere:** Solid outer Earth — crust + brittle upper mantle — Rocks, soil with nutrients; most rigid, coolest Earth layer; rocks elastic but not viscous; Earth structure: Crust, Mantle, Outer Core, Inner Core
- **Biosphere:** Living organisms + interactions with other segments — Divided into **ecosystems**; **Producers** (green plants, CO₂→O₂), **Consumers** (herbivores→carnivores, food chain), **Decomposers** (bacteria/fungi recycle nutrients to soil)

- Atmosphere evolution: 1st atmosphere (He, H) → 2nd atmosphere (CO₂, H₂O, NH₃, ~100× current gas) → rain/oceans dissolved CO₂ → life raised O₂, ozone layer formed
- Atmosphere functions: unequal heating, heat zones, temperature variation, pressure changes, winds, clouds, rainfall, snowfall
- Hydrological cycle: ocean evaporation exceeds sea precipitation by 10%; excess precipitates on land balancing the cycle

**Atmosphere Evolution (Detailed)**

- **1. Original atmosphere:** Helium and hydrogen from Earth's interior heat and solar radiation
- **2. Second atmosphere:** Volcanic eruptions released steam, CO₂, ammonia; ~100× more gas than today; virtually no oxygen
- **3. Water vapour condensed** → rain formed oceans → first aquatic environment favoured origin of life
- **4. Life evolved** → oxygen levels rose significantly → CO₂ levels lowered
- **5. Ozone layer formed** (O₃ allotrope of O₂) → protected life from UV radiation
- **6. Present atmosphere:** Oxygen-nitrogen composition supporting diverse life forms

**For Exam**

The four environmental segments are atmosphere (gases — N₂ 78%, O₂ 21%), hydrosphere (all water forms including cryosphere — 71% coverage), lithosphere (solid crust and upper mantle with soil nutrients), and biosphere (living organisms in ecosystems with producers, consumers and decomposers). They are mutually interdependent. The hydrosphere drives the hydrological cycle exchanging water between sea, atmosphere, land and organisms.

#### 1.1.4 Hydrological Cycle

**Theory**

The hydrological cycle is a continuous exchange of water between sea, atmosphere, land and living organisms through evaporation, cloud formation and precipitation. Land and water surfaces lose water by evaporation powered by solar energy. Ocean evaporation exceeds rainfall into seas by 10%; this excess precipitates on land, maintaining global water balance.

**Important Points**

- **1. Evaporation** — Water → vapour from oceans, lakes, rivers, soil. Solar energy drives evaporation; oceans contribute most atmospheric moisture
- **2. Transpiration** — Plants release water vapour. Water absorbed by roots returned to atmosphere through leaf pores
- **3. Cloud formation** — Water vapour condenses. Cooling causes condensation into clouds and fog in atmosphere
- **4. Precipitation** — Rain, snow, hail fall to Earth. Excess 10% ocean evaporation precipitates over land balancing cycle
- **5. Surface runoff** — Water flows over land. Collects in streams, rivers, lakes, catchment areas, reservoirs
- **6. Infiltration** — Rain seeps into soil. Water percolates downward forming groundwater in aquifers
- **7. Capillary action** — Groundwater rises. Maintains continuous water supply to surface soil layer
- **8. Plant absorption** — Roots take up soil water. Used in photosynthesis; returned via transpiration
- **9. Animal consumption** — Animals drink water. Returned to atmosphere through excretion and evaporation
- **10. Return to ocean** — Rivers and runoff. Surface and groundwater eventually discharge back to seas

- Cycle is balanced and continuous between Earth's surface and atmosphere
- Cryosphere (glaciers, ice caps) stores frozen hydrosphere; melting feeds rivers
- Disturbing any step (deforestation ↑ transpiration loss, urbanization ↓ infiltration) disrupts the cycle
- **PYQ (Q30 — another name for water cycle):** **Hydrologic cycle / Hydrological cycle** (SLM Objective Answer)

- **PYQ Q88 For Exam:** The **hydrological (hydrologic) cycle** continuously exchanges water between oceans, atmosphere, land and organisms through **evaporation** (solar-driven; oceans main source), **transpiration** (plants release vapour), **condensation** (cloud formation), **precipitation** (rain/snow), **surface runoff**, **infiltration** and **groundwater movement**. Ocean evaporation exceeds precipitation by 10%; excess falls on land. Deforestation and urbanisation disrupt the cycle.

**For Exam**

The hydrological cycle exchanges water continuously between oceans, atmosphere, land and organisms through evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, runoff, infiltration and groundwater movement. Ocean evaporation exceeds sea precipitation by 10%, with the excess falling on land. Rain infiltrates soil as groundwater, rises by capillary action, is absorbed by plants and animals, and returns to the atmosphere — completing the cycle.

#### 1.1.2 Atmosphere Layers

**Theory**

Atmosphere is stratified into layers differing in composition, temperature and radiation effects. SLM identifies three major layers (Troposphere, Stratosphere, Ionosphere) plus Mesosphere, Thermosphere and Exosphere. Troposphere holds 70% atmospheric mass and is the zone of weather; stratosphere contains the ozone layer; thermosphere/ionosphere has charged particles from photoionisation.

**Important Points**

- **Troposphere:** 8–18 km above surface — 70% atmospheric mass; 1% water vapour, more CO₂; dusty; temp falls 1°C/km (15°C to −56°C); zone of turbulence, climate, weather; ends at **tropopause**; most pollutants stay here; CFCs can cross upward
- **Stratosphere:** Up to 80 km — 1000× less water vapour, 1000× more ozone than troposphere; cloud-free; jet planes fly here; temp rises with altitude (−52°C to −2°C); **ozone layer** protects from UV; O₃ formed by UV/lightning + O₂; CFCs cause ozone depletion → sunburn, skin/eye cancers, cataracts; ends at **stratopause**
- **Mesosphere:** Up to 80 km above stratosphere — Temp decreases uniformly; may reach −100°C; very low pressure
- **Thermosphere/Ionosphere:** Up to 700 km — Very low density; temp rises rapidly (1200°C at 350 km); photoionisation → charged particles; also called **ionosphere**
- **Exosphere:** Up to 10,000 km — Only H and He atoms; very high temperature from solar radiation; **outermost layer**

- **PYQ Q80 For Exam:** Earth's atmosphere has five main layers: **Troposphere** (8–18 km, 70% mass, weather zone, temp decreases 1°C/km); **Stratosphere** (up to 80 km, ozone layer absorbs UV, CFCs cause depletion); **Mesosphere** (up to 80 km, −100°C); **Thermosphere/Ionosphere** (up to 700 km, ionised particles); **Exosphere** (up to 10,000 km, outermost, H and He only).

**For Exam**

The troposphere (8–18 km) contains 70% of atmospheric mass and is the weather zone where most pollutants remain. The stratosphere (up to 80 km) has the ozone layer protecting life from UV radiation; ozone depletion by CFCs causes skin cancers and cataracts. The thermosphere/ionosphere (up to 700 km) has ionised particles. The exosphere (up to 10,000 km) is the outermost layer with only hydrogen and helium.

#### 1.1.3 Weather and Climate

**Theory**

Weather consists of short-term atmospheric changes (minutes to months) over a limited area — sunshine, rain, winds, hail, floods, heat waves. Climate is the average weather over a long period (usually 30 years) for a larger region. Climate is what you expect; weather is what you get.

**Important Points**

**Weather vs Climate**

| Aspect | Weather | Climate |
|---|---|---|
| **Duration** | Short (minutes to months) | Long period average (typically 30 years) |
| **Area** | Small / local | Large / continent-scale |
| **Change** | Changes frequently | Relatively permanent |
| **Elements** | One element may dominate | Collective effect of all elements |
| **Memory tip** | What you get | What you expect |

- Climate study examines averages of precipitation, temperature, humidity, sunshine, wind, fog, frost, hail over long periods
- Climate changes affect people worldwide; greenhouse effect warms Earth's surface by trapping solar radiation

- **PYQ Q89 For Exam:** **Weather** is short-term atmospheric conditions (minutes to months) over a small/local area — changes frequently. **Climate** is the average weather over a long period (typically 30 years) over a large region — relatively permanent. Memory tip: climate = what you expect; weather = what you get. Key differences: duration, areal extent, and stability.

**For Exam**

Weather is the study of short-duration atmospheric conditions over a limited area and changes frequently. Climate is the average weather observed over a long period (usually 30 years) over a larger area and is relatively permanent. An easy distinction: climate is what you expect (hot summer), weather is what you get (hot day with thunderstorms).

### Unit 2: Natural Resources

#### 1.2.1 Concepts and Types of Natural Resources

**Theory**

Natural resources exist independent of human actions and include air, sunlight, water, soil, stone, plants, animals and fossil fuels. The five most important are air, water (71% Earth covered, only 2% fresh), soil, iron and forests. Based on availability, resources are classified as renewable (infinite, reusable — forest, wind, water) or non-renewable (limited, exhaustible — fossil fuels, minerals).

**Important Points**

**Renewable vs Non-renewable Resources**

| Type | Availability | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Renewable** | Infinite quantity; usable repeatedly | Forest, wind, water, solar, tidal energy |
| **Non-renewable** | Limited abundance; may run out | Petroleum, coal, minerals, fossil fuels |

- Five key natural resources: Air, Water, Soil, Iron, Forests
- Only 2% of Earth's water is fresh water
- Forests provide clean air and preserve global ecology
- Petroleum is non-renewable and gets exhausted with use

**For Exam**

Natural resources are materials existing independent of human actions, including air, water, soil, plants, animals and fossil fuels. Renewable resources (forest, wind, water) are available repeatedly; non-renewable resources (fossil fuels, minerals) are limited and exhaustible. Human necessities, comforts and luxuries all come from nature, but reckless overuse threatens both nature and mankind.

#### 1.2.2–1.2.4 Importance, Threats and Conservation

**Theory**

Natural resources are essential for human survival and development — air, land, solar energy, oil/gas, minerals, forests, industrialization and dams. Major threats are overpopulation, over-exploitation, climate change and environmental pollution. Conservation means protection and skilful management for future generations through reduce-reuse-recycle, afforestation, alternative energy and strict laws.

**Important Points**

- **Importance:** air to breathe; land for cultivation; solar energy; oil/gas for fuel; minerals (coal, iron, gold, diamonds); forests for timber/fuel/shelter; dams for flood control, irrigation, fishing, electricity
- **Threats:** (1) Overpopulation (2) Over-exploitation (3) Climate change (4) Environmental pollution
- **Conservation methods:** 3R (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle); community cleaning; rainwater harvesting; pond/lake maintenance; dams/reservoirs; stop deforestation; efficient resource use; public awareness; alternative energy; tree plantation; pollution control (electric vehicles); strict preservation laws

**Natural Resource Conservation (Detailed)**

- **1. Reduce, Reuse, Recycle (3R)** — minimise waste and maximise resource reuse
- **2. Volunteer community cleaning** — local participation in environment protection
- **3. Rainwater harvesting** — collect and store rain for future use; recharge groundwater
- **4. Pond and lake maintenance** — regular cleaning and desilting of water bodies
- **5. Dams and water reservoirs** — store water for irrigation, power and flood control
- **6. Stop deforestation** — prevent reckless tree cutting and forest fires
- **7. Efficient resource use** — avoid wastage of water, energy and raw materials
- **8. Public awareness** — educate communities on importance of resource conservation
- **9. Alternative energy** — shift from fossil fuels to solar, wind, biogas
- **10. Tree plantation** — afforestation and reforestation programmes
- **11. Pollution control** — electric vehicles, cleaner industrial processes
- **12. Strict laws** — implement and enforce natural resource preservation legislation

### Unit 3: Forest Resources

#### 1.3.1 Types and Classification of Forests in India

**Theory**

Forests are important renewable resources. India's forests were first classified in 1936 by Sir H.G. Champion; Champion and Seth (1968) classified them into 16 types under 6 major groups based on climate, physiognomy, species composition, phenology, topography, soil, altitude and biotic factors. India has ~24.16% forest cover with 16 forest types; forest cover reduced from ~30% (early 20th century) to 21.34% (2015).

**Important Points**

**Six major groups — 16 forest types (Champion & Seth, 1968)**

**Group I — Moist Tropical Forests**

- **1. Tropical Wet Evergreen** — Rainfall 2000–3000+ mm; Western Ghats, NE India, Andaman-Nicobar; dense 30–45 m canopy, high species diversity
- **2. Tropical Semi-evergreen** — Rainfall 1500–2500 mm; transition zones adjoining evergreen; 13.79% of Indian forests; multi-strata 24–36 m
- **3. Tropical Moist Deciduous** — Rainfall 1000–2000 mm; TN, Arunachal, Assam, Meghalaya, WB, Odisha; 19.73% of forests; shed leaves in winter
- **4. Littoral and Swamp** — Coastal/mesic; Sundarban (largest mangrove, 40% WB); Rhizophora, Avicennia; Casuarina on beaches

**Group II — Dry Tropical Forests**

- **5. Tropical Dry Deciduous** — Rainfall 800–1200 mm; largest type (38.2%); Kanyakumari to Himalaya foothills; dry teak (south), dry sal (north)
- **6. Tropical Thorn** — Rainfall 200–800 mm; Rajasthan, Punjab, Gujarat, MP, UP; Acacia, Prosopis, Ziziphus; open short thorny trees
- **7. Tropical Dry Evergreen** — Coastal low rain; Karnataka coast, AP east coast; Manilkara hexandra, Memecylon edule; 9–12 m height

**Group III — Montane Subtropical Forests**

- **8. Subtropical Broad-leaved Hill** — Altitude 1000–2000 m; Nilgiris, Palani, Tirunelveli; Quercus, Castanopsis (NE hills); Calophyllum (south)
- **9. Subtropical Pine** — Altitude 1000–1800 m; central/western Himalaya; Pinus roxburghii (chir pine); Haryana, HP, J&K, Punjab, Uttarakhand
- **10. Subtropical Dry Evergreen** — Foothills; Bhabar, Shiwalik; Olea cuspidata, Acacia modesta in J&K scrub forests

**Group IV — Montane Temperate Forests**

- **11. Montane Wet Temperate** — Altitude 1800–3000 m, rainfall >2000 mm; Eastern Himalaya; moss-covered short trees
- **12. Himalayan Moist Temperate** — Altitude 1500–3300 m; Kashmir, HP, Uttarakhand, Sikkim; oak, Cedrus deodara, Abies, Picea
- **13. Himalayan Dry Temperate** — Altitude 1700–3000 m, rainfall <1000 mm; Inner Himalaya; Pinus gerardiana, Juniperus; Ladakh, Chamba, Garhwal

**Group V — Sub-Alpine Forests**

- **14. Sub-Alpine Forests** — Above 3000 m; Rhododendron, Betula utilis; J&K, HP, Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Arunachal, WB

**Group VI — Alpine Forests**

- **15. Moist-Alpine Scrub** — Up to 5500 m; Rhododendron + birch scrub; Kumaun, eastern Himalaya 3350–4600 m
- **16. Dry-Alpine Scrub** — Rainfall <370 mm; Juniperus, Lonicera, Potentilla; J&K, HP, Uttarakhand, Arunachal 3000–4600 m

**Other key facts**

- Champion classified forests first in 1936; revised survey 1968 by Champion & Seth
- 2015 FSI: very dense 2.61%, moderately dense 9.59%, open 9.14%, scrub 1.26%
- Mizoram highest % cover (88.93%); MP largest total area (77,462 km²); Lakshadweep 84.56%
- New classification (CFRE 2013): 10 major groups, 48 sub-groups; blue pine shifted to 2700 m elevation
- Average 26,000 sq. km forests converted to agricultural land annually in India

- **PYQ Q120 For Exam:** Indian forests (Champion & Seth, 1968) are classified into **6 major groups, 16 types**: **(I) Moist Tropical** — wet evergreen (2000–3000+ mm), moist/semi-evergreen; **(II) Dry Tropical** — dry deciduous (largest, 38.2%), thorn, littoral/mangrove (Sundarban); **(III) Montane Subtropical**; **(IV) Montane Temperate**; **(V) Sub-Alpine**; **(VI) Alpine scrub**. India has ~24.16% forest cover; declined from ~30% to 21.34% by 2015.

**For Exam**

India's forests (Champion & Seth, 1968) are classified into 6 major groups and 16 types ranging from tropical moist to alpine scrub. Tropical dry deciduous forests are the largest (38.2%). India has ~24.16% forest cover. Wet evergreen forests occur in high-rainfall Western Ghats/NE regions; Sundarban is the world's largest mangrove. Forest cover declined from 30% to 21.34% by 2015.

#### 1.3.2–1.3.4 Threats — Mining, Dams and Environmental Impact

**Theory**

Forest ecosystems face over-exploitation, timber extraction, deforestation, mining and large dams. Over 72% of IUCN Red List species are threatened by over-exploitation; 62% by agriculture. Mining degrades ~80,000 hectares in India; dams (1550+ large dams, max in Maharashtra) submerge forests, displace tribals and alter river ecosystems. Nehru called dams "Temples of modern India" but they cause irreparable environmental damage.

**Important Points**

- **Deforestation:** Clearance or permanent destruction of forest cover by human activities — a serious form of environmental degradation
- **Deforestation causes:** reckless tree cutting, forest fires for plantation, air pollution
- **Mining effects:** land degradation, top soil loss, drying of springs/streams, 33% forest decline in mining areas; Goa lost 50,000+ ha; Jharia/Raniganj/Singrauli deforestation

- **PYQ Q46 For Exam:** **Deforestation** is the clearance/destruction of forests by human activities — a serious form of environmental degradation. Main causes: **(1) reckless and ruthless cutting of trees**, **(2) forest fires set for plantation**, **(3) air pollution**. Forests store carbon, support biodiversity and stabilise climate; deforestation destroys these functions, increases soil erosion, landslides and global warming.
- **Dams — 6 major impacts:** (1) Downstream morphology/water quality change (2) Forest/biodiversity loss (~40,000 km² flooded worldwide) (3) Salinity and pollution (4) Reservoir-induced earthquakes (Koyna 1967) (5) Design failures (Machu dam Gujarat 1979) (6) Resettlement of environmental refugees (mostly tribals)
- India: 1550+ large dams; Maharashtra >600, Gujarat >250, MP 130

**Mining Effects (Detailed)**

- Shallow deposits: surface mining; deep deposits: sub-surface mining — both degrade land, lose topsoil
- ~80,000 hectares under mining stress in India
- Dries perennial springs and streams in mountainous areas
- Mussoorie and Dehradun valleys: large-scale deforestation from mining
- Forest area declined 33% in mining zones; increased landslide risk
- Goa: >50,000 ha forest destroyed since 1961; Jharia, Raniganj, Singrauli (Jharkhand) extensive deforestation
- Khirakot, Kosi valley, Almora: 14 ha lost to magnetite/soapstone mining
- Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka: radioactive mineral mining threatens forests
- Western Ghats: copper, chromite, bauxite, magnetite mining projects threaten rich forests

**Large Dam Impacts (Detailed)**

- **1.** Downstream rivers lose sediment → coastal erosion; fisheries decline if runoff reduced >25%
- **2.** ~40,000 km² forest/wetland flooded worldwide; catchment deforestation → siltation
- **3.** Massive evaporation → salinity increase; delta areas face sea water ingress
- **4.** Koyna dam (Maharashtra 1967): reservoir-induced earthquake; Tehri dam controversy in Garhwal
- **5.** Machu dam burst (Gujarat 1979): poor construction caused widespread flooding
- **6.** Tribal displacement without participation or benefit-sharing; incomplete land records

**For Exam**

Threats to forests include over-exploitation, timber extraction, deforestation, mining and dams. Mining degrades land and forests (80,000 ha stressed in India). Large dams submerge forests, displace tribals as environmental refugees, alter downstream ecosystems, increase salinity, and can trigger earthquakes. Deforestation by reckless cutting, forest fires and pollution destroys carbon-stabilizing forest ecosystems globally.

#### 1.3.5–1.3.7 Conservation, Social Forestry and Agroforestry

**Theory**

Conservation preserves natural assets for future generations maintaining species, ecosystem and nutrient cycling diversity. Strategies include afforestation, reforestation, social forestry and agroforestry. Afforestation creates new forests on historically non-forest land; reforestation restores recently cleared forest areas. Social forestry (NCA 1976, NFP 1988) plants trees outside conventional forests for local community needs. Agroforestry integrates trees, crops and livestock on the same land.

**Important Points**

- **Afforestation:** New forest on land with no prior forest — One sapling per tree; increases forest area
- **Reforestation:** Re-establishing forest on cleared forest land — Two saplings per felled tree; costlier
- **Social Forestry:** Forestry outside conventional forests for local fuel, fodder, timber — Government programme; roadsides, wastelands, community lands
- **Agroforestry:** Trees + crops/livestock on same plot — Farmer's own land; shelter belts; nutrient cycling

- Conservation: increase plantation area, alternative materials, reserved timber forests, remote sensing monitoring, fire fighting, enforce anti-poaching laws, Joint Forest Management (WB, Haryana 1970s)
- Social forestry benefits: fuel, fodder, timber, income, community cohesion, waste land reclamation, wind/dust protection, desertification check
- Agroforestry benefits: soil fertility, erosion control, moisture retention, nutrient cycling, N-fixing trees, income diversification

**Social Forestry vs Agroforestry**

| Aspect | Social Forestry | Agroforestry |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Tree planting outside conventional forests for community benefit | Integration of trees, crops and/or livestock on the same land |
| **Land** | Vacant land, roadsides, railway tracks, panchayat lands, religious places | Farmer's own field/own land |
| **Objective** | Fuel wood, fodder, timber, wind/water erosion control | Maximise biomass per unit area; food + fodder + fuel wood |
| **Scale** | Massive government programme on community/wasteland | Localized unit of land management |
| **Philosophy** | "Forestry of the people, by the people, for the people" | Rural self-sufficiency through complementary crop-tree production |
| **Recognition** | NCA 1976, National Forest Policy 1988 | — |
| **Examples** | Wind breaks on dry farms, trees along canals/streams | Cocoa, coffee, rubber, oil palm with shade trees; shelter belts |

**For Exam**

Forest conservation includes afforestation (new forests on unused land), reforestation (restoring cleared forests), social forestry (community tree planting on non-forest land for local needs) and agroforestry (integrating trees with crops/livestock). Other strategies include alternative timber substitutes, remote sensing for deforestation hotspots, fire control, strict laws and Joint Forest Management involving local communities.

### Unit 4: Water Resources

#### 1.4.1–1.4.2 Global Distribution and Water Resource Types

**Theory**

Earth's ~332.5 million cubic miles of water is extremely unevenly distributed — over 97% saline (oceans), only 3% freshwater. Of freshwater on land, 79% locked in ice/glaciers, 20% groundwater, only 1% easily accessible surface freshwater. Water exists as saltwater, surface water, groundwater, frozen water (cryosphere) and atmospheric vapour.

**Important Points**

- **Oceans and Seas** — 97.4%: Saline; Na⁺, Cl⁻ dominant ions
- **Ice Caps and Glaciers** — 1.98%: Largest freshwater store; 90% in Antarctica
- **Groundwater and Soil water** — 0.599%: Aquifers; Great Artesian Basin Australia
- **Lakes** — 0.007%: 52% of accessible surface freshwater
- **Atmosphere** — 0.001%: Water vapour, clouds
- **Rivers** — 0.0001%: Smallest but vital; use judiciously

- **Surface water:** rivers, lakes, ponds, wetlands; replenished by precipitation
- **Groundwater:** subsurface pore space/aquifers; natural input = seepage; output = springs, ocean seepage
- **Estuary:** brackish water where fresh meets salt water; rich in organisms
- **Frozen water:** glaciers; if all melted sea rises ~6 m (20 ft); Himalayas = "Roof of the World"
- Desalinization removes salts but is costly and energy-intensive

**For Exam**

Over 97% of Earth's water is saline in oceans; only 3% is freshwater, mostly locked in ice caps (79% of land freshwater). Rivers constitute only 0.0001% but are critical for human use. Water resource types include surface water (rivers, lakes), groundwater (aquifers), estuaries (brackish), frozen water (glaciers/cryosphere) and atmospheric vapour. Sustainable management requires judicious river water use.

#### 1.4.4–1.4.7 Threats, Water Logging, Floods and Droughts

**Theory**

Water threats are quality or quantity issues from man-made or natural sources. Groundwater threats include landfills, septic systems, mining, agriculture (nitrates/pesticides), urbanization (impervious surfaces, saltwater intrusion). Surface water faces point source (single identifiable) and non-point source (multiple diffuse) pollution. Water logging saturates soil restricting air; floods and droughts are major natural hazards.

**Important Points**

- **Groundwater threats (4 groups):** waste disposal, resource extraction, agriculture, urbanization
- **Surface pollution sources:** domestic sewage, industrial waste, agricultural sediments/fertilizers/pesticides
- **Pollution effects:** cholera, jaundice, typhoid; kills aquatic life; artificial eutrophication (excess P, N → low O₂)
- **Treatment:** primary (mechanical solids removal) → secondary (biological bacteria) → tertiary (advanced)
- **Water logging types:** natural/human-induced; temporary/permanent; rainfed/irrigated; agricultural/urban
- **Flood types:** flash, single event (2–3 days rain), multiple event (Indo-Gangetic), seasonal, coastal, estuarine, dam failure, snow/glacier melt
- **Drought types:** meteorological (low precipitation), agricultural (crop failure), hydrological (low reserves in aquifers/lakes)

**Groundwater Threats (Detailed)**

- **Waste disposal:** Landfills leach toxins; septic systems release bacteria, viruses, nitrates, solvents
- **Mining:** Intersects aquifers, lowers water tables, increases iron, manganese, sulphate in wells
- **Agriculture:** Over-applied manure/fertilizer → nitrates ("blue baby" disease in infants); pesticides
- **Urbanization:** Impervious surfaces (roofs, roads) block recharge; over-pumping draws saltwater into coastal aquifers
- **Natural:** Dissolved solids, calcium carbonate (hardness), corrosive acidity from rock leaching

**Surface Water Threats (Detailed)**

- **Point source:** Single identifiable — factory discharge, sewage plant outlet (easier to regulate)
- **Non-point source:** Diffuse — agricultural fertilizers, herbicides, sedimentation from farmland (harder to control)
- **Domestic sewage:** Homes and businesses pollute rivers and lakes
- **Industrial waste:** Chemical pollutants from refineries, manufacturing into freshwater
- **Agricultural pollution:** Sediments, fertilizers, pesticides, animal wastes → eutrophication

**Causes of Water Pollution (SLM — PYQ Q79)**

- **Domestic sewage** — homes and businesses discharge into rivers/lakes
- **Industrial waste** — chemicals from refineries and manufacturing (point source)
- **Agricultural runoff** — fertilizers, pesticides, sediments (non-point source)
- **Groundwater contamination** — landfills, septic systems, mining, nitrates, urban saltwater intrusion
- **Effects:** cholera, jaundice, typhoid; kills aquatic life; eutrophication (excess N, P → low O₂)

- **PYQ Q79 For Exam:** Water pollution is caused by **domestic sewage** and **industrial waste** (point sources — single identifiable outlets), **agricultural runoff** of fertilizers, pesticides and sediments (non-point/diffuse sources), **landfill leachates** and **septic systems** contaminating groundwater, **mining** intersecting aquifers, and **urbanization** (impervious surfaces blocking recharge, saltwater intrusion). Effects include waterborne diseases (cholera, typhoid, jaundice), death of aquatic life, and artificial eutrophication from excess nitrogen and phosphorus.

**Major Threats to Water Resources (SLM — PYQ Q103)**

- **Quantity threats:** over-extraction, droughts, climate change reducing supply, population growth
- **Quality threats:** groundwater and surface water pollution (see above)
- **Natural hazards:** floods, water logging saturating soil
- **Human causes:** industrialization, urbanization, agricultural modernization, poor wastewater treatment

- **PYQ Q103 For Exam:** Major threats to water resources include **groundwater contamination** (landfills, septic systems, mining, agricultural nitrates/pesticides, urbanization reducing recharge and causing saltwater intrusion), **surface water pollution** (domestic sewage, industrial discharge, agricultural non-point runoff), **water scarcity** from overuse and climate change, **water logging** (soil saturation), **floods** and **droughts** affecting both quantity and quality of freshwater.

**For Exam**

Groundwater is threatened by landfills, septic systems, mining, agricultural nitrates and urbanization reducing recharge. Surface water pollution comes from point sources (factory discharge) and non-point sources (agricultural runoff). Floods result from heavy rainfall, storm surges and dam failures; droughts from prolonged below-normal precipitation affecting soil moisture, crops and groundwater reserves.

#### 1.4.8–1.4.9 Conservation and Coastal Regulation Zone

**Theory**

Water conservation manages water as a sustainable resource through traditional harvesting, watershed management, groundwater recharge and wastewater reclamation. Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) under Environment Protection Act 1986 (first notification February 1991, MoEF) regulates activities within 500 m of High Tide Line (HTL) and 100 m along tidal creeks/estuaries.

**Important Points**

**Conservation methods (detailed):**

- **1. Traditional water harvesting** — Johad (Rajasthan), Talab, Saza Kuva, Paar systems; suited to local conditions
- **2. Watershed management** — managing land area shedding water into a region; sustainable distribution of water, soil, plant and animal resources
- **3. Pani Panchayat** — voluntary farmer groups regulating river-to-field water supply for optimum crop use
- **4. Percolation ponds** — check dams tapping rainwater; reduce water shortage by recharging aquifers
- **5. Spring water trapping** — new solution to conserve and harvest natural spring flows
- **6. Rooftop rainwater harvesting** — rain from building roof collected into well/tank below; **Tamil Nadu made compulsory for all houses**
- **7. Wastewater reclamation** — treating wastewater to acceptable quality; direct reuse (linked processing) or indirect (discharge to stream/aquifer then mixed)
- **8. Groundwater recharge** — artificial injection of water through wells into deep aquifers where surface application is ineffective
- **9. Check dams and farm ponds** — slow runoff, increase percolation in rural areas
- **10. Desalinization** — removing salts from seawater; costly but essential for arid coastal regions
- **CRZ categories (2019):** CRZ-I (ecologically sensitive — mangroves, corals, dunes; IA/IB split), CRZ-II (urban developed, >50% built-up), CRZ-III (rural; IIIA/IIIB with NDZ 50–200 m), CRZ-IV (aquatic; IVA seaward 12 nm, IVB tidal water bodies)
- CRZ 2019: MoEFCC clearance only for CRZ-I and IV; State clearance for CRZ-II/III; 20 m NDZ for islands
- India major freshwater source: groundwater; rooftop harvesting recharges groundwater

**Flood Types (Detailed)**

- **Flash floods:** Cloud burst, violent convectional storms; little warning — Short duration, great volume; biggest human life loss
- **Single event:** 2–3 days widespread heavy rain — Cyclonic disturbances, storms, depressions
- **Multiple event:** Successive weather disturbances — Indo-Gangetic plains
- **Seasonal:** Monsoon patterns — South India: winter monsoon floods; others: summer monsoon
- **Coastal:** High tides, storm surges, hurricanes — Beach/soil erosion; tsunamis as coastal killer waves
- **Estuarine:** Tidal flooding in river mouths — Brackish water zone flooding
- **Dam failure:** Poor construction or earthquake damage — Machu dam Gujarat 1979
- **Snow/glacier melt:** Sudden warming melts ice — Mountainous regions; August 2021 Maharashtra, West Bengal floods

**Drought Types**

| Type | Cause | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Meteorological** | Prolonged below-average precipitation | Usually precedes other drought types |
| **Agricultural** | Crop failure from irrigation/soil issues | Can occur even without rainfall change |
| **Hydrological** | Water reserves below threshold | Shows up slowly in aquifers, lakes, reservoirs |
- Droughts: second-most costly weather events after hurricanes
- Annual dry seasons in tropics increase drought and bush fire chances
- Heat waves worsen drought by accelerating evaporation

**CRZ Details (2019 Notification)**

- Coastal land up to 500 m from HTL + 100 m along tidal creeks, estuaries, backwaters, rivers
- CRZ-I A: mangroves, corals, sand dunes, salt marshes, national parks, sea grass beds (11 sensitive areas)
- CRZ-I B: intertidal zone between Low Tide Line and High Tide Line
- CRZ-II: developed urban coastal areas; floor space index norms de-freezed in 2019
- CRZ-III A: population density >2161/km² (2011 census) — NDZ 50 m; CRZ-III B: <2161/km² — NDZ 200 m
- CRZ-IV A: water area seaward up to 12 nautical miles; CRZ-IV B: tidal water bodies up to 5 ppt salinity
- Promotes sustainable coastal tourism; pollution abatement facilities in CRZ-IB

**For Exam**

Water conservation includes traditional harvesting (Johad, Talab), watershed management, rooftop rainwater harvesting, percolation ponds and groundwater recharge. CRZ notification (1991, amended 2003/2011/2019) regulates coastal activities within 500 m of HTL in four categories (CRZ-I to IV), with CRZ-I as ecologically sensitive and CRZ-III having No Development Zones of 50–200 m.

### Unit 5: Land and Mineral Resources

#### 1.5.1–1.5.2 Land Resources and Degradation

**Theory**

Land resources include hills, valleys, plains, river basins and wetlands essential for agriculture and terrestrial ecosystems. Land degradation is undesirable change in biophysical environment value from human-induced processes (excluding natural hazards). IPCC: ~25% ice-free land degraded; UN: ~30% land degraded globally, 12 million ha/year lost (size of Greece). SDG 15 targets land degradation-neutral world by 2030.

**Important Points**

- Land = essential for agriculture, survival, prosperity, ecosystem maintenance
- **Causes:** deforestation, clear-cutting, nutrient depletion, overgrazing, over-irrigation, urban sprawl, quarrying, vehicle off-roading, enlarged field sizes
- Up to 4% world's agricultural land seriously degraded
- Soil erosion rate 10–100× higher than soil formation rate (conventional tillage)
- Humankind must act as stewards, not exploiters, for unborn generations

**For Exam**

Land degradation is undesirable human-induced decline in land's biophysical value affecting agricultural productivity, environment and food security. Causes include deforestation, overgrazing, over-irrigation, urbanization and quarrying. About 30% of global land is degraded, losing 12 million hectares annually. SDG 15 aims for land degradation neutrality by 2030.

#### 1.5.3–1.5.5 Landslides, Soil Erosion and Desertification

**Theory**

Human-induced landslides (HIL) result from topography modification, water circulation changes, land use changes and infrastructure ageing. Soil erosion loses >36 billion tons fertile soil/year from agriculture. Desertification is reduction/destruction of land's biological potential creating desert-like conditions — affects ~25% of world's land; caused by human activities, not natural expansion of deserts.

**Important Points**

- **Landslide triggers:** deforestation, cultivation, construction, vibrations, blasting, mining, earthwork, logging, urbanization, LU/LC changes
- **Erosion types:** sheet (invisible, light soil), wind (visible), rill (heavy rain, hillside), gully (large channels), ephemeral (natural depressions)
- **Desertification causes:** over-cultivation, over-grazing, over-irrigation (salination), deforestation, mining rejects, population growth/land encroachment
- **Desertification consequences:** productivity loss, poverty, water resource drying, biotic/abiotic damage — largely irreversible
- Arthur Young: "God sleeps in minerals, awakens in plants, walks in animals, and thinks in man"

**Soil Erosion (Detailed)**

- >36 billion tons fertile soil lost annually from world agriculture
- Sediment from sheet/rill erosion enters streams and waterways
- Controlling sediment integral to soil management and water quality
- Conventional tillage: erosion rate 10–100× higher than soil formation rate
- Light-coloured soils indicate years of invisible sheet erosion damage

**Desertification (Detailed)**

- Affects ~25% of world's land area; fertile land near existing deserts most vulnerable
- **Over-cultivation:** cash crops displace traditional agriculture to marginal lands
- **Over-grazing:** cattle remove vegetation; wind blows away loose fertile soil particles
- **Over-irrigation:** waterlogging → salination → soil unfit for cultivation
- **Deforestation:** exposes topsoil to erosion by wind and water
- **Mining rejects:** disposal on fertile land causes total soil erosion
- **Population growth:** land encroachment increases pressure on fragile ecosystems
- Consequences largely irreversible; especially severe in developing countries

**Soil Erosion Control Methods (SLM — PYQ Q66)**

- **Afforestation/Reforestation** — tree cover binds soil; forests provide anti-erosion benefits (SLM 1.3.4–1.3.6)
- **Wind breaks / shelter belts** — social forestry on dry farmlands (SLM 1.3.6)
- **Vegetation cover maintenance** — prevents loose fertile soil particles blowing/washing away
- **Agroforestry** — trees + crops improve soil fertility and erosion control
- **Controlling sediment runoff** — integral to soil management (SLM 1.5.4)

- **PYQ Q66 For Exam:** Methods to **control soil erosion** include **afforestation and reforestation** (tree cover binds soil — SLM: forests provide anti-erosion and soil protection), **wind breaks/shelter belts** on farmlands (social forestry), **maintaining vegetation cover**, **agroforestry** (trees with crops), and **controlling sediment runoff**. Erosion types (sheet, wind, rill, gully) are reduced by vegetative cover and proper land management.

**For Exam**

Man-induced landslides result from deforestation, mining, construction and land use changes. Soil erosion (>36 billion tons/year lost) includes sheet, wind, rill and gully types. Desertification degrades ~25% of Earth's land through over-cultivation, over-grazing, over-irrigation, deforestation and mining — reducing productivity irreversibly and causing poverty in developing nations.

### Unit 6: Energy Resources

#### 1.6.1–1.6.2 Types and Classification of Energy

**Theory**

Energy is the capacity to perform work; total usable energy = energy supply. Chief sources: fossil fuels, flowing water, biomass, atomic minerals, plus solar, wind, tidal, chemical and geothermal. Sources classified as renewable (hydro, solar, wind — available repeatedly) or non-renewable (coal, oil, gas, nuclear, geothermal — permanently consumed). ~85% world commercial energy from coal, oil, natural gas.

**Important Points**

- **Availability:** Infinite; naturally replenished — Finite; permanently consumed
- **Examples:** Solar, wind, hydro, tidal, biogas — Coal, petroleum, natural gas, nuclear, geothermal
- **Environmental impact:** Low pollution; clean energy — High pollution; SO₂, NOₓ, CO₂, ash, radioactive waste
- **Cost:** High initial setup; low running cost — Lower setup historically; rising as reserves deplete
- **Depletion:** Never exhausted at human scale — Exhaustible; coal, oil, gas will run out
- **Regeneration time:** Immediate to seasonal (wind 5 months) — Millions of years (fossil fuels from prehistoric organisms)
- **Share of commercial energy:** Growing but <15% globally — ~85% (coal + oil + gas)
- **Sustainability:** Sustainable long-term — Unsustainable at current consumption rates
- **Geographic dependence:** Site-specific (solar needs sunlight, wind needs wind) — Widespread where deposits exist
- **By-products:** None/minimal (biogas → manure) — Fly ash, oil spills, mine waste, nuclear waste
- **India examples:** Solar parks (Rajasthan, Gujarat), wind (TN, Maharashtra), biogas (rural) — Coal (Jharia, Singrauli), oil (Bombay High), uranium (Jaduguda)

- **Fossil Fuels:** Coal, Oil (Petroleum), Natural Gas — 85% commercial energy; coal types: Anthracite > Bituminous > Sub-bituminous > Lignite; peat precursor; oil = 40% commercial energy; gas = 21%, cleanest fossil fuel (85% methane)
- **Solar & Wind:** Photovoltaic, solar thermal, windmills — Solar: unlimited, pollution-free; expensive cells; wind: 5-month generation, cheap power, heavy installation cost
- **Biogas & Tidal:** Organic waste methane, tidal dams — Biogas from sewage/manure; community/family plants; tidal power expensive, seasonal
- **Nuclear:** Uranium-235, Plutonium-239 fission — 17% world electricity; 1 kg U = 25,000 kg coal energy; waste disposal problem
- **Geothermal:** Earth's interior heat — Iceland, Italy, Japan, Philippines, NZ, USA; pipes + turbines
- **Hydrogen:** Future fuel/emissions-free carrier — Most abundant element; combusts to water vapour; hydrogen economy needs renewable electricity

- Coal: thermal power plants; low sulphur = less pollution; ash content affects efficiency; 1 MW plant needs ~1 acre for ash dump
- Clean energy: renewables + energy efficiency; biomass/large hydro have wildlife tradeoffs
- Less than 5% electricity from solar, wind, hydro currently; shift to renewables needed for hydrogen economy

**Coal Types**

| Coal Type | Carbon Content | Key Feature | Main Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anthracite** | Highest | Hardest, rarest; most heat | Premium fuel, steel industry |
| **Bituminous** | High | Most common industrial coal | Thermal power, coke production |
| **Sub-bituminous** | Medium | Less carbon than bituminous | Power generation |
| **Lignite** | Lowest | Brown coal; high moisture | Local power plants |
| **Peat** | Precursor | Partially decayed swamp matter | Precursor to lignite |

**Solar Energy (Detailed)**

- Two conversion methods: (1) Photovoltaic (solar cells — silicon crystals) (2) Solar thermal (copper pipe on blackened base under glass)
- Solar energy falling every 29 seconds = one day's human energy requirement
- Advantages: unlimited, no air/water/thermal/noise pollution, no disasters, conserves resources, technology available
- Disadvantages: expensive cells, sparse distribution, needs collection/concentration devices, cannot fully replace conventional fuels
- Applications: water pumping, refrigeration, solar ponds, building temperature control

**Wind Energy (Detailed)**

- Windmill blades → turbine → power generator
- Generation period: ~5 months (seasonal wind availability)
- 200 kW/250 kW generator produces 6–7 lakh units/year
- Merits: immediate power after commissioning, cost-free generation, low recurring cost, pollution-free, income source
- Demerits: heavy installation cost, expensive maintenance, seasonal availability

**Biogas & Nuclear (Detailed)**

- Biogas: methane from anaerobic decomposition of sewage, garbage, manure, crop residues in sealed containers
- Plant types: community, industrial, night soil, family size, improved chulhas
- Residue after biogas = high-quality organic manure
- Nuclear: 17% world electricity; U-235 and Pu-239 fission; 1 kg U = 25,000 kg coal; fuel bundles last 1–2 years
- Nuclear waste: radioactive liquids and gases severely affect environment; disposal is major challenge
- Geothermal: Iceland, Italy, Japan, Philippines, NZ, USA — pipes into wells connected to turbines
- Hydrogen: most abundant element; combusts to H₂O; PEM fuel cells (Honda FCX Clarity: 1 kg = 68 miles); needs renewable electricity for sustainable production

**Biogas — What it is & Energy Production (SLM 1.6.2.3 — PYQ Q92)**

- **Definition:** Biogas = methane (CH₄) gas produced/released from organic wastes — sewage, garbage, manure, crop residues
- **Process:** Anaerobic decomposition — wastes decompose in the **absence of air** inside sealed containers (similar to natural gas in origin)
- **Production setup:** Container filled with organic waste (mainly animal dung/excreta), closed at top with **gas tap** for trapping methane
- **Energy production:** Methane collected from tap → used as fuel for **cooking, lighting, and power generation** — converts waste into usable energy
- **By-product (manure):** After gas production, spent slurry = **high-quality organic manure** for croplands (dual benefit: energy + fertiliser)
- **Plant types:** Community biogas plants, industrial biogas plants, night soil biogas plants, family-size biogas plants, improved chulhas — operational worldwide
- **India:** Widely used in rural areas; Haritha Keralam promotes biogas plants for waste-to-energy
- **Advantages:** Renewable non-conventional source; uses waste; reduces pollution; low-cost rural energy; produces manure alongside fuel

- **PYQ Q92 For Exam:** Biogas is methane (CH₄) gas produced from organic wastes such as sewage, garbage, manure and crop residues through anaerobic decomposition (in the absence of air) — similar to natural gas. For energy production, organic wastes (mainly animal dung/excreta) are placed in a sealed container with a gas collection tap; bacterial decomposition releases methane which is trapped and used as fuel for cooking, lighting and power generation. After gas production, the spent material serves as high-quality organic manure for agriculture. Community, industrial, night soil and family-size biogas plants operate worldwide including rural India. Biogas is a renewable, non-conventional energy source that converts waste into useful fuel while reducing environmental pollution.

**For Exam**

#### 2.1.1.1–2.1.1.2 Abiotic and Biotic Components

**Theory**

Abiotic components are physical/non-living factors essential for organism survival — inorganic substances (CO₂, water, N, Ca, P in material cycles = standing state), organic compounds (proteins, carbohydrates synthesized by biota), climatic factors (rain, light, temperature, humidity, wind) and edaphic factors (minerals, soil, pH, topography). Biotic components are categorized by nutrition into producers, consumers and decomposers.

**Important Points**

- **Producers:** Photoautotrophs, chemoautotrophs — Green plants, algae, phytoplankton; photosynthesis/chemosynthesis; primary production; first food chain link
- **Consumers:** Primary, Secondary, Tertiary, Quaternary — Heterotrophs lacking chlorophyll; herbivores, carnivores, omnivores, detritivores; humans = secondary consumers (omnivores)
- **Decomposers:** Saprotrophs — Bacteria, fungi, worms, insects; break dead organic matter to inorganic nutrients for producers; complete nutrient recycling

- Standing state = amount of inorganic substances at given time in ecosystem
- Chemosynthetic bacteria (sulphur bacteria) synthesize food without sunlight
- Decomposers: bacteria, protozoa, earthworms; essential for nutrient recycling

**Consumer Types (Detailed)**

- **Primary consumers:** Producers (plants) directly — Zooplankton, butterflies, rabbits, giraffes, pandas, elephants, deer
- **Secondary consumers:** Primary consumers — Ants, snakes, rats, crabs, lions, blue whales (via krill), humans (omnivores)
- **Tertiary consumers:** Secondary consumers — Top carnivores feeding on smaller carnivores
- **Quaternary consumers:** Tertiary consumers — White shark, polar bear, alligator — apex of food chain
- **Herbivores:** Plants only — Cow, deer, caterpillar
- **Carnivores:** Other animals — Tiger, eagle, spider
- **Omnivores:** Both plants and animals — Bear, crow, human
- **Detritivores:** Dead organic matter — Earthworm, millipede, maggot

- **Biotic components:** Living organisms — **Producers, Consumers, Decomposers** (classified by mode of nutrition)
- **PYQ Q68 For Exam:** **Biotic components** are living parts of an ecosystem: **Producers** (autotrophs — green plants, algae, phytoplankton; photosynthesis); **Consumers** (heterotrophs — primary/herbivores, secondary/carnivores, tertiary/quaternary; e.g. deer, tiger); **Decomposers** (saprotrophs — bacteria, fungi, earthworms; recycle dead matter to inorganic nutrients). They form trophic levels in food chains.

- **PYQ Q94 For Exam:** **Abiotic components** are non-living factors: **(1) inorganic substances** — CO₂, water, nitrogen, minerals; **(2) organic compounds** — proteins, carbohydrates; **(3) climatic factors** — rainfall, temperature, humidity, light, wind; **(4) edaphic (soil) factors** — texture, pH, topography. Examples: sunlight, water, air, minerals, rocks, temperature. They regulate energy flow and nutrient cycling in ecosystems.

**For Exam**

#### 2.1.2–2.1.4 Food Chain, Food Web and Ecological Pyramids

**Theory**

A food chain is a linear sequence transferring nutrients and energy from producer through consumers to decomposers at trophic levels. Two types: grazing food chain (plants→herbivores→carnivores) and detritus food chain (dead organic matter→decomposers→carnivores). Food webs are interconnected food chains showing complex energy flow. Ecological pyramids (Charles Elton, 1927) graphically represent trophic relationships.

**Important Points**

- **Grazing food chain:** Starts with green plants; energy from photosynthesis; plants→herbivores→carnivores; most ecosystems follow this
- **Detritus food chain:** Starts with dead organic matter; decomposers→detritivores→carnivores; pyramid of numbers may be inverted
- **Food web:** Multiple interconnected chains; more complex = more sustainable; multiple energy flow paths
- **Pyramid of Numbers:** Organism count per trophic level; usually upright except detritus chains
- **Pyramid of Biomass:** Mass per trophic level; upright except oceans (zooplankton > phytoplankton biomass)
- **Pyramid of Energy:** Energy per trophic level; **always upright**; only ~10% energy transfers to next level (90% lost as heat, respiration)

**Ecological Pyramids — Types**

| Pyramid Type | Based On | Shape | Key Point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Numbers** | Organism count per trophic level | Usually upright; inverted in detritus chains | May be inverted in detritus food chains |
| **Biomass** | Total mass per trophic level | Upright on land; inverted in oceans | Zooplankton biomass > phytoplankton in oceans |
| **Energy** | Energy content per trophic level | Always upright | Only ~10% energy transfers to next level (Elton, 1927) |

- **PYQ Q90 For Exam:** Ecological pyramids (Charles Elton, 1927) graphically represent trophic relationships. **Three types:** **(1) Pyramid of Numbers** — organism count per trophic level; usually upright except detritus chains; **(2) Pyramid of Biomass** — mass per level; upright on land but inverted in oceans; **(3) Pyramid of Energy** — energy per level; **always upright** because only ~10% energy transfers to the next level (90% lost as heat and respiration).

- Trophic level = position in food chain (producers at bottom)
- Apex predators receive only ~0.01% of starting energy
- 10% law: only ~10% energy creates biomass at next trophic level

**Food Chain — Concept (SLM 2.1.2 — PYQ Q95)**

- **Definition:** Linear sequence of organisms where **nutrients and energy** are transferred from one organism to another (one organism consumes another)
- **Feeding relationship:** Shows order of events in ecosystem — who eats whom
- **Flow:** Begins with **producer** (autotroph) → passes through consumers at different **trophic levels** → ends with **decomposer**
- **Trophic levels:** Producers (bottom) → Primary consumers (herbivores) → Secondary consumers (carnivores) → Tertiary/quaternary consumers
- **Unidirectional:** Energy flows in a **single linear path** — not recycled back up the chain
**Grazing vs Detritus Food Chain**

| Type | Starts With | Energy Source | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Grazing food chain** | Green plants (producers) | Photosynthesis | Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk |
| **Detritus food chain** | Dead organic matter | Decomposers on detritus | Dead leaves → Fungi/Bacteria → Earthworm → Bird |
- **10% energy rule:** Only ~10% energy passes to next trophic level; 90% lost as heat, respiration, hunting effort

**Food Chain Examples**

- **Grazing (grassland):** Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk — Producer → Primary → Secondary → Tertiary → Quaternary
- **Grazing (aquatic):** Phytoplankton → Zooplankton → Small fish → Large fish → Shark — Producer → Primary → Secondary → Tertiary → Apex
- **Grazing (forest):** Tree leaves → Deer → Tiger — Producer → Primary herbivore → Apex carnivore
- **Detritus (forest floor):** Dead leaves → Fungi/Bacteria → Earthworm → Bird — Detritus → Decomposer → Detritivore → Carnivore

- **PYQ Q95 For Exam:** A food chain is a **linear sequence** of organisms through which nutrients and energy are transferred when one organism consumes another — showing feeding relationships in an ecosystem. It begins with **producers** (green plants/autotrophs) at the first trophic level, passes through **primary consumers** (herbivores), **secondary and tertiary consumers** (carnivores), and ends with **decomposers**. Two types: **(1) Grazing food chain** — starts with green plants; energy from photosynthesis; plants→herbivores→carnivores (most common); **(2) Detritus food chain** — starts with dead organic matter; decomposers→detritivores→carnivores. Energy flows **unidirectionally** along a single path; only ~10% transfers to the next level. Examples: Grass→Grasshopper→Frog→Snake→Hawk; Phytoplankton→Zooplankton→Fish→Shark; Dead leaves→Fungi→Earthworm→Bird.

**Food Web Examples**

- **Pond ecosystem:** Algae eaten by zooplankton, tadpoles and small fish; small fish eaten by large fish and birds; large fish eaten by snakes; all dead matter decomposed by bacteria — multiple interconnected chains
- **Grassland ecosystem:** Grass supports mice, rabbits, insects; mice/rabbits eaten by snakes, foxes, hawks; snakes also eat frogs that eat insects; hawk also scavenges — one organism in multiple chains
- **Ocean ecosystem:** Phytoplankton → krill → whale; phytoplankton → small fish → tuna → shark; seaweed → sea urchin → otter — complex cross-feeding
- Food web complexity increases ecosystem sustainability; single chain failure in web does not collapse entire system

**Ecological Pyramid — Numerical Example (10% Energy Rule)**

- Producers (plants): 100% energy captured from sun
- Primary consumers (herbivores): receive ~10% of producer energy
- Secondary consumers (carnivores): receive ~1% (10% of 10%)
- Tertiary consumers: receive ~0.1%
- Apex predators: receive only ~0.01% of starting energy
- 90% energy lost at each level through respiration, hunting, heat to surroundings
- This is why productivity/energy pyramids are always upright
- Explains why top predators are few in number compared to producers

- **PYQ Q110 For Exam:** **Food chain** = **linear sequence** transferring energy when one organism eats another — single path, unidirectional (producers→consumers→decomposers). **Food web** = **network of interconnected chains** — multiple paths, more complex and **more sustainable**. If one species is lost from a food web, alternate paths exist; a broken food chain collapses entirely. Food webs reflect real ecosystem feeding more accurately.

**For Exam**

Food chains transfer energy linearly through trophic levels: grazing chains start with producers, detritus chains start with dead matter. Food webs are interconnected chains providing more sustainable ecosystems. Ecological pyramids (Elton, 1927) show trophic relationships — numbers, biomass and energy types. Pyramid of energy is always upright because energy decreases ~90% at each level due to heat loss and respiration.

### Unit 3: Definition and Levels of Biodiversity

#### 2.3.1–2.3.3 Biodiversity and Its Levels

**Theory**

Biodiversity (coined 1985; Walter Rosen; Bio + diversity) describes richness and variety of life — essential for ecosystem health and sustainable livelihood. Measured by species richness (number of species) and species evenness (relative abundance). Three levels: genetic diversity (within species), species diversity (between species) and ecosystem diversity (between ecosystems). Alpha, beta and gamma diversity measure diversity at local, between-ecosystem and regional scales.

**Important Points**

- **Genetic:** Genetic characteristics within a species — Homo sapiens: Chinese, Indian, African; 98.4% genes shared with chimpanzees; gene pool for crop breeding
- **Species:** Species population/total organisms ratio — Decreases from equator to poles; tropics (23.5°N–23.5°S) richest; ~1.8 million documented species
- **Ecosystem:** Variation among ecosystems in a region — Natural vs modified; India exceptionally rich; forests, grasslands, deserts, aquatic, farmland
- **Alpha diversity:** Diversity within one ecosystem — Species richness of that area
- **Beta diversity:** Diversity between ecosystems — Species change between ecosystems
- **Gamma diversity:** Overall regional diversity — Diversity across all ecosystems in region

- **Species richness:** counts species only, not abundance
- **Species evenness:** Shannon-Weiner index (H, entropy), Simpson index (D, 1949, concentration)
- Hotspots = areas rich in species diversity; India among world's 15 exceptionally rich nations
- Genetic diversity prevents inbreeding extinction; wild relatives = gene pool for crops

**Biodiversity Measurement (Detailed)**

- **Species richness example:** Forest A and B both have 3 species (tigers, deer, rabbits) but Forest A has more even distribution → higher evenness
- **Shannon-Weiner index (H):** measures entropy/surprise in species distribution; higher H = more diverse
- **Simpson index (D, 1949):** measures concentration when individuals classified into types
- **Whittaker (1965):** divided diversity into alpha (within ecosystem), beta (between ecosystems), gamma (regional total)
- **Species diversity formula concept:** ratio of one species population to total organisms; 0 = infinite diversity, 1 = single species
- Tropics (23.5°N–23.5°S) have maximum species richness; decreases toward poles
- Natural undisturbed tropical forests >> plantation forests in species richness
- ~1.8 million species documented; estimated total 1.5–20 billion (most undiscovered)

**For Exam**

Biodiversity is the variety of life at genetic (within species), species (between species) and ecosystem (between ecosystems) levels. It is measured by species richness (species count) and species evenness (abundance distribution, using Shannon-Weiner or Simpson indices). Species diversity decreases from equator to poles; tropics harbour most species. India is among 15 biodiversity-rich nations with ~1.8 million known species.

#### 2.3.4 Values of Biodiversity

**Theory**

Biodiversity provides essential environmental services — nutrient cycling, soil formation, air/water cleansing, watershed protection, flood reduction. It also has direct and indirect value to humans through consumptive, productive, social, ethical, aesthetic and option values. Only a tiny fraction of known species has been investigated for food, medicinal or industrial potential.

**Important Points**

- **Consumptive use:** Direct utilization by local communities — Timber for housing, fuel wood for cooking, fodder for cattle, fish by fisherfolk, medicinal plants (neem, tulsi)
- **Productive use:** Commercial/industrial exploitation — Bioprospecting for drugs (Rauvolfia for blood pressure), rubber/oil palm plantations, wild crop relatives for rice/wheat breeding
- **Social values:** Cultural and community dependence — Traditional multi-crop farming as insurance against crop failure; sacred groves (deorais) preserving gene banks; tribal forest livelihoods
- **Ethical/moral:** Right of all species to exist — Indian sacred groves in tribal areas; lion in Hinduism, elephant in Buddhism, eagle in Egyptian civilization
- **Aesthetic:** Beauty, tourism, recreation — Wildlife safaris (Kaziranga rhinos), bird watching (Bharatpur), coral reef diving; forest meditation and eco-tourism
- **Option value:** Future potential not yet realized — Wild rice varieties for drought resistance; Asian wild gaur for cattle breeding; undiscovered medicinal plants in tropical forests

- Biodiversity services: nutrient cycling, soil formation, air/water cleansing, watershed protection, flood reduction, pollination
- Only tiny fraction of ~1.8 million known species investigated for food, medicinal or industrial potential
- Biological prospecting = searching wild genetic properties for crop/livestock/drug development

### Unit 5: Threats to Biodiversity

#### 2.5.1–2.5.3 Habitat Loss, Poaching and Man-Wildlife Conflict

**Theory**

Biodiversity faces habitat destruction, invasive species, climate change, pollution and overexploitation. Habitat destruction is the primary threat — reducing biodiversity when organisms lose homes. Poaching is the second biggest direct threat after habitat loss. Man-wildlife conflict arises from human-wildlife negative interactions affecting both sides, intensified by population growth.

**Important Points**

- **Main threats:** habitat loss/degradation/fragmentation, invasive alien species, climate change, nutrient pollution, overexploitation
- **Habitat loss:** forests/grasslands turned to deserts/wastelands; tropical forests losing ~1%/year → 5–10% species reduction in 30 years
- **Poaching:** illegal hunting for trade — tiger skin/bones, elephant ivory, rhino horns, musk deer perfume, orchids, Rauvolfia, Datura
- UN 2015 resolution on illicit wildlife trafficking; SDG targets on poaching
- **Man-wildlife conflict (WWF):** negative impact on human social/economic/cultural life and wildlife conservation
- **Consequences:** habitat destruction, injury/death, crop damage, livestock depredation, property damage, population decline, trophic cascades
- Scientists estimate ~10 million species lost by 2050; ~25% world species may extinct rapidly

**Man-Wildlife Conflict (Detailed)**

- WWF definition: interaction causing negative impact on human social/economic/cultural life AND wildlife conservation
- As old as civilization but intensified by population growth in recent centuries
- **Causes:** habitat encroachment, crop raiding by elephants/monkeys, livestock depredation by predators, property damage
- **Consequences:** injury/death of humans and wildlife, crop damage, livestock loss, property destruction, population decline, trophic cascades
- **India examples:** elephant crop raids, tiger/leopard livestock attacks, monkey urban nuisance, wild boar crop damage
- Mangrove clearing for prawn farming → reduced marine fish breeding habitat
- Wetland drainage for agriculture → lost biodiversity and water regulation
- Scientists estimate human activities may eliminate ~10 million species by 2050
- 25% of world species could become extinct within 20–30 years from habitat loss and overexploitation

**For Exam**

Major biodiversity threats are habitat destruction (primary — forests, wetlands, coral reefs), poaching (second biggest — tigers, elephants, rhinos for illegal trade) and man-wildlife conflict from population growth and land use changes. Habitat loss reduces biodiversity as species cannot survive without ecosystems. Poaching for skins, ivory, horns and medicinal plants drives species toward extinction. Human activities may eliminate 25% of world species within decades.

#### 2.5.4 Species Categories and Red Data Book

**Theory**

IUCN (1948) classifies species by extinction risk. Red Data Book (origin: Russian Federation, 1961–1964) records endangered/rare species with colour-coded threat levels. Key concepts include endemic species (unique geographic area), keystone species (disproportionate ecosystem impact), foundation species (dominant primary producers) and flagship species (charismatic conservation symbols).

**Important Points**

- **Rare:** Infrequently encountered; IUCN "at risk" but not endangered — Found in isolated locations; ~75% known species potentially rare
- **Threatened:** Vulnerable to near-future extinction — IUCN categories: Vulnerable, Endangered, Critically Endangered
- **Critically Endangered (CR):** Highest risk; 80% population decrease in 3 generations — Extremely high extinction risk
- **Endangered:** Serious extinction danger if negative factors continue — Giant panda, tiger, blue whale, Asian elephant, snow leopard
- **Vulnerable (VU):** High extinction risk unless threats improve — IUCN category
- **Extinct:** No doubt last individual died — Dodo, passenger pigeon
- **Endemic:** Found only in specific geographic area — Opposite of cosmopolitan distribution
- **Keystone:** Loss/addition causes major ecosystem changes — Tiger, lion, crocodile, elephant; bats pollinating ebony
- **Foundation:** Dominant primary producer — Kelp in kelp forests, corals in coral reefs
- **Flagship:** Charismatic species representing conservation cause — Indian tiger, African elephant, giant panda

**IUCN Threat Categories**

| Category | Code | Risk Level |
|---|---|---|
| **Critically Endangered** | CR | 80% population decline in 3 generations; highest risk |
| **Endangered** | EN | High extinction risk if threats continue |
| **Vulnerable** | VU | High risk unless conditions improve |
| **Near Threatened** | NT | Close to qualifying for threatened categories |
| **Least Concern** | LC | Widespread and abundant |
| **Extinct** | EX | No doubt last individual died |

**Red Data Book Colour Codes**

| Colour | Status |
|---|---|
| **Black** | Extinct |
| **Red** | Endangered |
| **Amber** | Vulnerable |
| **White** | Rare |
| **Green** | Recovering |
| **Grey** | Insufficient data |
- **Extinct in Wild:** EW; Only survives in captivity; Some plant/animal species

**Special Species Designations**

- **Endemic:** Found only in one geographic region — Lion-tailed macaque (Western Ghats only)
- **Native/Indigenous:** Naturally occurs without human introduction — Indian peafowl in India
- **Keystone:** Disproportionate ecosystem impact — Tiger (regulates prey populations), bats (pollinate ebony)
- **Foundation:** Dominant primary producer shaping ecosystem — Kelp in kelp forests, corals in reefs
- **Flagship:** Charismatic species for conservation campaigns — Indian tiger, African elephant, giant panda, leatherback turtle
- **Rare:** Infrequently encountered; ~75% known species potentially rare — Giant otter, Sumatran rhino, vaquita, red wolf

**For Exam**

IUCN classifies threatened species as Vulnerable, Endangered or Critically Endangered (80% decline in 3 generations). The Red Data Book records rare/endangered species with colour codes (black=extinct, red=endangered, amber=vulnerable). Endemic species exist only in specific areas; keystone species (tiger, elephant) disproportionately affect ecosystems; flagship species (tiger, panda) promote public conservation support.

#### 2.6.2–2.6.4 In-situ Conservation — National Parks, Sanctuaries, Biosphere Reserves

**Theory**

In-situ (on-site) conservation protects organisms in natural habitats through protected areas — cost-effective, large-scale, allows natural evolution. Three types: National Parks (strict protection), Wildlife Sanctuaries (limited human activity) and Biosphere Reserves (multi-purpose with core/buffer/transition zones, UNESCO MAB Programme 1971).

**Important Points**

**Protected Areas Comparison**

| Aspect | National Park | Wildlife Sanctuary | Biosphere Reserve |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Strict conservation of natural environment | Protect wild animals and habitat | Multi-purpose: wildlife + tribal life + domesticated species |
| **Human activities** | Prohibited — no grazing, forestry, cultivation | Limited — timber/collection if not harming conservation | Zoned — varies by core/buffer/transition |
| **Boundaries** | Well demarcated by government | Less rigidly defined | Large area with three zones |
| **Tourism** | Allowed (public recreation) | Allowed (recreation) | Allowed (research + tourism) |
| **Commercial use** | Not permitted | Not for animal breeding/commercial sale | Sustainable activities in transition zone |
| **Research** | Limited | Biologists permitted for study | Core = strict; buffer = research/education |
| **Tribal rights** | Generally excluded | Some traditional rights may continue | Transition zone protects tribal lifestyle |
| **Legal status** | Highest protection under Wildlife Act | Protected but less strict than NP | UNESCO MAB Programme (1971); 18 in India |
| **Examples** | Corbett, Kanha, Kaziranga, Gir, Periyar | Bharatpur, Chilika, Periyar, Madumalai | Nilgiri, Sundarbans, Gulf of Mannar, Nanda Devi |
| **India count** | 100+ national parks | Many sanctuaries nationwide | 18 biosphere reserves |

#### 2.6.5 Ex-situ Conservation and Conservation Projects

**Theory**

Ex-situ (off-site) conservation protects endangered species outside natural habitats — zoos, botanical gardens, gene banks, seed banks, field gene banks, cryopreservation (−196°C liquid nitrogen). Advantages: longer breeding time, reintroduction possible, genetic techniques applicable. India launched Project Tiger (1973, WWF), Crocodile Breeding (1975) and Project Elephant (1992).

**Important Points**

- **Botanical gardens/Zoos:** Conventional ex-situ; housing, breeding, reintroduction; educational value
- **Gene banks:** Store crop genetic resources; monitor viability; regeneration; IPGRI/FAO protocols
- **Seed banks:** Most cost-effective long-term storage; minimizes genetic erosion
- **Field gene banks:** Living collections for perennials/recalcitrant species; educational and research access
- **Cryopreservation:** −80°C (CO₂) or −196°C (N₂); prevents ice crystal damage; stabilizes cells
- **Tissue culture:** In-vitro conservation biotechnology method

- **Project Tiger (1973):** 9 reserves (16,339 km²) → 27 reserves (37,761 km²); tigers 268→1500; protects habitat not just species
- **Crocodile Conservation (1975):** 30+ breeding centres; restocked 20 water bodies; most successful ex-situ project
- **Project Elephant (1992):** 12 states; viable elephant populations in natural habitats
- Ex-situ advantages: controlled breeding, reintroduction, genetic preservation
- Best preservation: in-situ network of National Parks + Sanctuaries covering all ecosystems

**Conservation Projects (Detailed)**

- **Project Tiger:** 1973; Govt of India + WWF; 9 → 27 tiger reserves; 16,339 → 37,761 km²; Tigers: 268 (1972) → ~1500 (1997); protects entire habitat
- **Crocodile Breeding:** 1975; Govt of India; 30+ captive breeding centres, zoos; All 3 species bred; thousands restocked in 20 water bodies
- **Project Elephant:** 1992; Govt of India; 12 states (north, NE, south India); Long-term viable elephant populations; habitat still shrinking

**Ex-situ Methods (Detailed)**

- **Gene banks:** store, maintain, reproduce crop varieties and wild relatives; periodic regeneration when viability declines
- **Seed banks:** most cost-effective; IPGRI/FAO protocols; large populations, minimal genetic erosion
- **Field gene banks:** for perennials and recalcitrant species; susceptible to disease and disasters
- **Cryopreservation:** −196°C liquid nitrogen; cells stabilized; prevents chemical reactions; widely used for in-vitro material
- **Botanical gardens/zoos:** housing, breeding, reintroduction, education
- **Tissue culture:** biotechnological in-vitro conservation method
- Convergent evolution: sharks and dolphins (similar form, different ancestors)
- Divergent evolution: Darwin's finches (common ancestor, different beak adaptations)

**India's Fragile Ecosystems Requiring Protection**

- **Mountain ecosystems:** Extremely fragile; forest cover loss → severe soil erosion, river course changes
- **Island ecosystems:** Andaman-Nicobar unique species; any human activity → rapid extinction
- **Evergreen forests:** Species-rich but highly vulnerable to logging and conversion
- **Coral reefs:** Sensitive to pollution, temperature rise, ocean acidification
- **River/wetland ecosystems:** Seriously affected by pollution and surrounding land use changes
- **Elephant habitats:** Require large diverse areas — open grasslands (post-monsoon) + forest foliage (dry season)
- Protected area for elephants must include multiple habitat types for complete species complement

- **PYQ Q116 For Exam:** **Ex-situ conservation** protects species **outside** natural habitats. **Plants:** botanical gardens, **seed banks**, field gene banks, **cryopreservation** (−196°C), tissue culture. **Animals:** zoos, captive breeding. India: **Project Tiger (1973)**, **Crocodile Breeding (1975)** — most successful ex-situ, **Project Elephant (1992)** — 12 states. Allows controlled breeding, genetic techniques and reintroduction.

**For Exam**

Ex-situ conservation removes species from threatened habitats to zoos, botanical gardens, gene banks, seed banks or cryopreservation (−196°C). It allows controlled breeding and reintroduction. India's Project Tiger (1973) protects tiger habitats across reserves; Crocodile Breeding (1975) successfully restocked waterways; Project Elephant (1992) covers 12 states. In-situ protected area networks remain the best approach for preserving total ecosystem diversity.

#### 3.1.1 Environment and Human Health

**Theory**

Human health and well-being are intimately linked to the state of the environment. Good quality natural environments provide clean air, water, fertile land, and energy inputs; environmental degradation significantly increases the risk of cancer, heart disease, asthma, and many other illnesses. Global climate change will have wide-ranging health impacts — negative impacts are anticipated to outweigh positive ones through changes in temperature extremes, floods, droughts, infectious disease occurrence, food production, air pollutants, and population displacement.

- IAOH (Indian Association of Occupational Health) and NIOH (National Institute of Occupational Health, Ahmedabad) promote occupational health

#### 3.1.2 Environmental Degradation and Health Hazards

#### 3.1.5 Occupational Diseases

**Theory**

Occupational disease is any illness associated with a particular occupation, resulting from biological, chemical, physical, and psychological factors in the work environment. Major occupational diseases of concern in India include silicosis, asbestosis, musculoskeletal injuries, coal workers' pneumoconiosis, byssinosis, pesticide poisoning, and noise-induced hearing loss. Common environmental diseases also include asbestosis, silicosis, fluorosis, asthma, and allergies.

**Important Points**

- **Asbestosis:** Prolonged inhalation of asbestos fibres (mining, construction) — Chronic fibrotic lung disease; pulmonary fibrosis; can occur from secondhand exposure
- **Silicosis:** Inhaling crystalline silica dust (glass manufacturing, tunnel work, stone cutting) — Progressive scarring of lung tissue; cough, sputum, breathlessness; acute silicosis from massive single exposure
- **Fluorosis:** Excess fluoride via drinking water/food/industrial pollutants over long period — Crippling disease; dental staining to skeletal fluorosis (joint stiffness, bone changes); low fluoride prevents caries
- **Asthma:** Environmental allergens, air pollution, dust mites, mould, occupational chemicals, tobacco smoke — Airways narrow and swell; wheezing, coughing; urbanization increases prevalence
- **Allergies:** Airborne allergens (dust mites, pollens, fungi, animal dander); urbanization and vehicle emissions — Allergic rhinitis in ~40% of world population; environmental pollutants worsen allergies

- IAOH = association of health professionals promoting occupational health
- NIOH = WHO collaborative centre for occupational health, Ahmedabad

- **PYQ Q111 For Exam:** **Occupational diseases** from workplace exposure. Examples: **Silicosis** (silica dust — mining/construction); **Asbestosis** (asbestos fibres — lung fibrosis); **Pesticide poisoning** (agriculture); **Fluorosis** (excess fluoride — dental/skeletal); **Asthma** (occupational chemicals); **Allergies** (industrial dust/fumes). Prevention: PPE, ventilation, safe limits, monitoring. **NIOH** (Ahmedabad) promotes occupational health in India.

**For Exam**

Occupational diseases result from workplace exposure to harmful agents. In India, major concerns include silicosis, asbestosis, and pesticide poisoning. Asbestosis is chronic lung fibrosis from asbestos fibre inhalation. Silicosis results from silica dust causing lung scarring in mining/construction workers. Fluorosis is a crippling disease from excess fluoride affecting teeth and bones. Asthma involves narrowed airways triggered by pollution and allergens. Allergies are worsened by urbanization, vehicle emissions, and airborne allergens affecting 40% globally with allergic rhinitis.

### Unit 2: Epidemiological Issues in Health

#### 3.2.1 Epidemiology and Health Issues

**Theory**

Epidemiology studies the frequency and pattern of health events in populations — how many cases occur relative to population size. It researches disease causation, transmission, outbreak investigation, surveillance, and treatment comparisons. The Epidemiologic Triad consists of an external agent, a host, and an environment — optionally including a vector (e.g., Anopheles mosquito transmitting malaria plasmodium; Ixodes ticks transmitting Lyme disease).

**Important Points**

- Epidemiology = study of disease frequency, distribution, and determinants in populations
- Major areas: disease causation, transmission, outbreak investigation, surveillance, occupational epidemiology
- Epidemiologic Triad: **Agent + Host + Environment** (± Vector)
- Malaria: plasmodium stored in mosquito salivary glands, injected into next human host
- Epidemiologists use biology, statistics, social sciences, and engineering for exposure assessment

**For Exam**

Epidemiology studies the frequency and pattern of health events in defined populations. It addresses disease causation, transmission, surveillance, and outbreak investigation. The Epidemiologic Triad comprises agent, host, and environment — with vector as optional fourth element. Example: Anopheles mosquito vector transmits malaria plasmodium without being affected itself. Epidemiology relies on biology, statistics, and social sciences to understand and control disease spread.

#### 3.2.2–3.2.3 Water-Related and Water-Borne Diseases

**Theory**

Water-related diseases include those from direct/indirect water contact, ingestion, water aerosols, vector habitats, and water shortage during floods/droughts. Water-borne diseases involve direct transmission via microbiological or chemical pollutants. Seven categories exist: waterborne microbiological, waterborne chemical, water hygiene, water contact, water vector habitat, excretal disposal, and water aerosol diseases. About 502,000 diarrheal deaths annually linked to contaminated water; 2 billion people use fecally contaminated drinking water sources.

**Important Points**

- **Cholera:** Vibrio cholerae (bacteria); Contaminated water/food; Severe diarrhoea, dehydration; rapid outbreak after floods
- **Diarrhoea:** Multiple pathogens; Fecally contaminated water; Most common water-borne disease; mainly affects children
- **Typhoid:** Salmonella typhi (bacteria); Contaminated drinking water; Fever, abdominal pain; common in areas with poor sanitation
- **Amebiasis:** Entamoeba histolytica (protozoa); Contaminated water; Dysentery, liver abscess
- **Hepatitis:** Hepatitis A/E viruses; Contaminated water; Liver inflammation; jaundice
- **Gastroenteritis:** Bacteria/viruses; Contaminated water; Vomiting, stomach cramps, diarrhoea
- **Giardiasis:** Giardia lamblia (protozoa); Contaminated water; Diarrhoea, abdominal cramps
- **Campylobacteriosis:** Campylobacter (bacteria); Contaminated water; Diarrhoea, fever
- **Scabies:** Sarcoptes scabiei (mite); Water contact; Skin infestation
- **Worm infections:** Helminths; Contaminated water/soil; Intestinal parasites; soil colonization from contaminated irrigation

**Seven Categories of Water-Related Disease**

- **1.** Waterborne microbiological disease
- **2.** Waterborne chemical disease
- **3.** Water hygiene disease
- **4.** Water contact disease
- **5.** Water vector habitat disease
- **6.** Excretal disposal disease
- **7.** Water aerosol disease

- WHO: 844 million lack basic drinking water; 159 million depend on surface water; 2 billion use fecally contaminated sources
- Climate-linked outbreaks: heavy rains overflow sewage plants; drought concentrates pathogens in limited water
- Prevention: good engineering, water management, personal hygiene, antibacterial/anti-parasitic treatment
- Water-borne diseases mainly affect children due to poor hygiene and weak immunity

**For Exam**

Water-related diseases encompass all illnesses from water exposure — ingestion, contact, aerosols, vectors, and shortage. Water-borne diseases are directly transmitted by pathogens in water. Major examples: cholera (Vibrio cholerae), diarrhoea (most common), typhoid (Salmonella typhi), hepatitis, giardiasis, and worm infections. Seven categories of water-related disease are recognized. WHO reports 502,000 annual diarrheal deaths; 2 billion use contaminated water. Climate change increases outbreaks — floods contaminate water, droughts concentrate pathogens. Prevention requires engineering, hygiene, and effective water management.

#### 3.2.4–3.2.5 Vector-Borne and Air-Borne Diseases

**Theory**

Vectors are living organisms transmitting pathogens between humans or from animals to humans during blood meals. Airborne diseases spread via pathogenic microbes discharged through coughing, sneezing, or close contact, remaining suspended on dust particles and respiratory droplets. Over one billion people are infected annually from vector-borne diseases; airborne diseases like TB and influenza spread rapidly in crowded, poorly ventilated settings.

- **Vector-borne:** Dengue; Aedes mosquito; Virus via blood meal; Substantial economic burden; thrives in poor housing
- **Vector-borne:** Lyme disease; Ixodes tick; Borrelia via blood meal; Example in Epidemiologic Triad
- **Vector-borne:** Yellow fever; Mosquito; Virus via blood meal; Tropical regions
- **Vector-borne:** Schistosomiasis; Freshwater snails; Parasite in water habitat; Water vector habitat disease
- **Vector-borne:** Leishmaniasis; Sand flies; Parasite via blood meal; Kala-azar in India
- **Vector-borne:** Lymphatic filariasis; Mosquito; Worm via blood meal; Elephantiasis
- **Air-borne:** Tuberculosis; Mycobacterium tuberculosis; Cough/sneeze droplets; Bacterial; poor ventilation increases spread
- **Air-borne:** Influenza; Influenza virus; Respiratory droplets; Seasonal epidemics
- **Air-borne:** Measles; Paramyxovirus; Cough/sneeze aerosols; Highly contagious
- **Air-borne:** Chickenpox; Varicella zoster virus; Airborne droplets; Viral exanthem
- **Air-borne:** COVID-19; SARS-CoV-2; Respiratory aerosols; Pandemic potential
- **Air-borne:** Whooping cough; Bordetella pertussis; Cough droplets; Bacterial; affects children
- **Air-borne:** Common cold; Rhinovirus; Droplet/contact; Most frequent respiratory infection
- **Air-borne:** Anthrax; Bacillus anthracis spores; Airborne spores; From soil/garbage sources
- **Air-borne:** Meningitis; Bacteria/virus/fungus; Airborne particles; Brain/spinal cord inflammation

- Vectors: mosquitoes, ticks, flies, sand flies, fleas, freshwater snails
- Over 1 billion infected and 1 million+ die annually from vector-borne diseases

Environmental movements are social/political movements for conservation or environmental improvement — also called green or conservation movements. #### 3.4.2.2 Haritha Keralam Mission

**Theory**

Haritha Keralam Mission (10 November 2016) is a people-centric endeavour under Nava Kerala Mission, envisioned by the Government of Kerala to bestow an eco-friendly land with greenery and serenity to posterity. Despite receiving twice the national average rainfall, Kerala loses bulk rainwater to the sea within 48–72 hours, making water conservation critical. The mission uses a biocentric approach placing humans within the greater natural environment context.

**Important Points**

- **Launch:** 10 November 2016; under Nava Kerala Mission umbrella
- **Slogan:** To safeguard the natural resources
**Three sub-missions:**

- **Hygienic Waste Management:** Effective waste disposal at source — Decentralized disposal (Thumboormoozhy model); biogas plants; block-level plastic/e-waste collection; hospital waste disposal; centralized disposal in Trivandrum, Kochi, Kozhikode
- **JalaSamirdhi (Water Conservation):** Rejuvenate water bodies — Phase 1: wells, ponds, springs, canals, streams; Phase 2: rivers, reservoirs, lakes, backwaters; dead river revival; rainwater harvesting; groundwater recharging; 3Rs (Reduce, Reserve, Recycle)
- **Sujalam Suphalam (Agriculture):** Organic farming development — Organic vegetables in all households; paddy on barren lands; rooftop cultivation; sapling planting; zero pesticide cultivation

- **Threats addressed:** Water depletion, unscientific waste management, air pollution, chemical effluents in water, deforestation, grass cover loss
- **Goals:** Eco-friendly environment, sustainable natural resources, zero pesticide food, biodiversity protection, social hygiene
- **Implementation:** Through Local Self-Government bodies in people-centric mode

### Unit 5: Population Growth, Population Explosion and Associated Issues

#### 3.5.1 Population Growth

**Theory**

Population is growing rapidly, outpacing the planet's support capacity. Population impacts environment through natural resource use and waste production. In India, rapid growth threatens environment through agricultural expansion, uncontrolled urbanization, industrialization, and habitat destruction, declining per capita agricultural land, forest, and water resources.

**Important Points**

- Malthusian theory: population grows geometrically, resources arithmetically
- Overpopulation = existing population exceeds Earth's carrying capacity
- Population growth factors: birth rate, death rate, immigration, emigration
- Population is development source but environmental degradant when exceeding threshold limits
- Poverty is both cause and effect of environmental degradation

### Unit 6: Sustainable Development

#### 3.6.1–3.6.3 Sustainable Development: Concept and Principles

**Theory**

The concept of sustainable development was described by the **1987 Brundtland Commission Report** (*Our Common Future*) as:

> **"Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."**

Sustainable development is a way for people to use resources without the resources running out. There are four dimensions — society, environment, culture, and economy — which are intertwined, not separate. Sustainability balances environmental, societal, and economic considerations in pursuit of improved quality of life. Three main pillars: **Economic, Environmental, and Social** — informally referred to as **people, planet, and profits**.

**Important Points**

- **Brundtland Commission (1987):** World Commission on Environment and Development (WCED)
- **Full definition:** Present needs met without compromising future generations' ability to meet their own needs
- **Three pillars:** Economic growth, Environmental protection, Social equity (people, planet, profits)
- **Four dimensions:** Society, Environment, Culture, Economy
- **Main features:** (i) respect all life forms, (ii) improve human life quality, (iii) minimise resource depletion, (iv) enable communities to care for environment
- **Six principles:** Conservation of ecosystem; sustainable society; biodiversity conservation; population control; human resource development; public participation
- Criticised as vague by some; others feared it meant reversion to pre-modern lifestyles

**For Exam**

Sustainable development (Brundtland Commission, 1987) is defined as: "Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." #### 4.1.2–4.1.5 Environmental Ethical Perspectives

**Theory**

Four major ethical perspectives govern how humans relate to the environment. Anthropocentrism places humans as primary moral agents; biocentrism extends moral standing to all living beings; ecocentrism values entire ecosystems including abiotic factors; ecofeminism links the exploitation of women and nature under patriarchal ideology. **Important Points**

**Environmental Ethical Perspectives**

| Perspective | Key Idea | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Anthropocentrism** | Humans are primary moral agents; superior to nature | Human-centred; nature as life-support system for humans |
| **Biocentrism** | All life deserves equal moral consideration (Paul Taylor, 1986) | Living organisms; interdependence of species |
| **Ecocentrism** | Ecosystems (living + nonliving) have inherent value | Nature-centred; holistic biodiversity |
| **Ecofeminism** | Nature and women linked through patriarchal exploitation (d'Eaubonne, 1974) | Gender–nature link; reproductive/nurturing role |

- Anthropocentrism: humans as "roof and crown" of creation — led to environmental destruction
- Biocentrism: two moral concerns — avoiding harm to sentient beings + preserving nature's purity
- Ecocentrism: most holistic; considers abiotic factors (sea levels, ocean acidity, weather patterns)
- Ecofeminism: reconceptualize men-women-nature relations in non-hierarchical ways

**For Exam**

### Unit 2: Overview of Solid Waste Segregation and Management

#### 4.2.1–4.2.2 Solid Waste Management and Segregation

**Theory**

Solid waste is unwanted garbage from industrial, residential, and commercial activities. Solid Waste Management (SWM) collects, treats, and disposes this waste through six functional components. **Waste segregation at source** is critical — the **3-bin system** separates waste into three categories for efficient recycling, composting, and disposal. The **3Rs (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle)** are the core waste-management principles for minimising waste and conserving resources (SLM also adds **Refuse** in zero-waste approach).

**Important Points**

**3Rs in Waste Management (SLM — PYQ Q83)**

- **Reduce:** Minimise waste generation at source — consume less, avoid unnecessary products, reduce consumption and discards; fewer products are made when people buy less
- **Reuse:** Use items again before discarding — reusing discards; products made to last; extends product life and reduces waste volume
- **Recycle:** Recover usable materials from waste to manufacture new goods — keeps waste out of landfills and incinerators; provides recycled raw materials instead of virgin resources
- **Refuse (4th R — Zero Waste):** Refuse unnecessary packaging and single-use items; say no to waste-generating products
- **3R goal:** Conserve natural resources; reduce pollution from extraction, manufacturing and disposal; support zero waste and sustainable living
- **SLM principle:** "Reduce, Reuse, Recycle and Refuse are what we need for a safe and healthy living"
- **PYQ Q83 For Exam:** The 3Rs in waste management are **Reduce, Reuse, and Recycle** — fundamental strategies for sustainable solid waste management (SLM adds **Refuse** for zero waste). **Reduce** means minimising waste at source by consuming less and avoiding unnecessary products — fewer products are made when people buy less. **Reuse** means using items repeatedly before discarding — reusing discards and making products last longer. **Recycle** means recovering materials from waste to make new products, keeping waste out of landfills and incinerators and supplying recycled raw materials to manufacturers. Together, 3Rs conserve natural resources, reduce pollution from extraction and disposal, and support zero waste alongside source segregation, composting and safe disposal.

**3-Bin Segregation System**

- **Green bin:** Wet / Organic waste; Food scraps, vegetable peels, garden waste, biodegradable matter; Composting, biogas, vermicomposting
- **Blue bin:** Dry / Recyclable waste; Paper, cardboard, plastic bottles, metal cans, glass; Recycling, reuse
- **Red bin:** Hazardous / Reject waste; Sanitary waste, diapers, batteries, e-waste, medical waste, broken glass; Incineration, sanitary landfill, special treatment

- Segregation at source prevents mixed waste contamination and enables efficient processing
- Eco-clubs promote: source separation of waste, disposing to nearest storage point
- Swachh Bharat and Haritha Keralam emphasise waste disposal at source of generation

**Six Functional Components of SWM**

- **1** — **Waste generation**: Identifying and gathering unusable materials
- **2** — **Onsite handling, storage, processing**: Containers (household bins, communal drums, shallow pits) before collection
- **3** — **Waste collection**: Placing bins, collecting waste, loading collection vehicles
- **4** — **Waste transfer and transport**: Moving waste to regional disposal site (hand-cart, animal-cart, truck)
- **5** — **Waste processing and recovery**: Recovering reusable/recyclable materials
- **6** — **Disposal**: Final systematic disposal — landfilling, composting, incineration, recycling

- SWM = effective measures for managing plant, animal, and human waste
- Collection intervals must prevent storage facility overload

Six components: generation → handling → collection → transport → processing → disposal.

#### 4.2.3–4.2.4 On-Site and Off-Site Disposal

**Theory**

On-site disposal handles waste at the generation site. Off-site disposal transports waste to external facilities. Both approaches must consider local conditions, community safety, and environmental impact.

**Important Points**

- **On-site: Communal pit:** Direct disposal into fenced pit — 6 m³ per 50 people; cover weekly with soil; max 100 m from dwellings
- **On-site: Family pit:** Shallow pit (up to 1 m) per family — Best for large plots with organic waste; cover regularly
- **On-site: Communal bins:** Oil drums (halved, perforated base) — Prevents wind scatter and animal access
- **Off-site: Landfilling:** Waste in ground pit/trench, covered daily — 0.5 m soil cover; fenced sites; consultation with local authorities
- **Off-site: Incineration:** Burning combustible waste off-site — Reduces volume; best for pathogen destruction; cover ash with soil
- **Off-site: Composting:** Organic waste decomposition — Adds humus to soil; central management needs expertise
- **Off-site: Recycling:** Reuse of plastic, tins, glass — Strong tradition in developing countries reduces waste volumes

**For Exam**

On-site disposal manages waste at source: communal pits (6 m³/50 people), family pits (shallow, organic waste), and communal bins (perforated oil drums). Off-site options include landfilling (daily soil cover, 0.5 m), incineration (destroys pathogens, reduces volume), composting (organic waste to fertilizer), and recycling (plastic, tins, glass). Incineration is best for complete pathogen destruction. Landfill gases include CO₂ and methane.

#### 4.2.5 Zero Waste

**Theory**

Zero Waste is a philosophy eliminating materials with no viable end-of-use management — designing products and processes to avoid and eliminate waste volume and toxicity. It conserves resources and reduces pollution from extraction, manufacturing, and disposal through reduce, reuse, recycle, and refuse strategies.

**Important Points**

- Zero Waste = ethical, economical, efficient, visionary goal for sustainable natural cycles
- All discarded materials designed to become resources for others
- No burning or burying; conserve and recover all resources
- Strategies: reusing discards, reducing consumption, producer accountability, comprehensive recycling/composting, citizen participation, ban on incineration/illegal dumping, reducing landfilling over time
- Reduce + Reuse = fewer products made; products made to last; recycling keeps waste from landfills

#### 4.2.6–4.2.7 Plastics and Microplastics

**Theory**

Plastics are polymeric materials (long chains of monomers) used widely due to low cost, durability, and chemical resistance. Microplastics are particles <5 mm ("mermaid's tears") from primary sources (deliberately manufactured — cosmetics, toothpaste) or secondary sources (breakdown of larger plastics, synthetic fibres, tyre wear). They pass through water filtration, enter oceans, and harm aquatic and human health.

**Important Points**

- **Primary microplastics:** Deliberately manufactured <5 mm — Facial cleansers, toothpaste, microbeads (PE); ~6% EU skin-cleaning products contain microplastics
- **Secondary microplastics:** Breakdown of larger plastics — Degraded bags/bottles; synthetic clothing fibres from washing; tyre wear fragments
- **Effects on wildlife:** Entanglement (79% fatal) and ingestion (4% fatal) — Digestive blockage, reduced nutrition, starvation; gill lodging reduces respiration
- **Human health:** Ingestion of microplastics — Physical blockage, chemical additive leakage, POP adsorption; effects not fully understood

- IUPAC: plastics = polymeric materials with additives for performance/cost
- Found globally: Arctic, Antarctic, oceans, rivers, lakes, soil, atmosphere (indoor + outdoor)
- Entanglement far more fatal (79%) than ingestion (4%) for marine organisms

**For Exam**

Plastics are synthetic polymers widely used but persist in environment. Microplastics (<5 mm) are primary (manufactured for cosmetics/hygiene — 6% of EU skin products) or secondary (degraded larger plastics, clothing fibres, tyre fragments). They pass water filters, enter oceans and food chains, causing entanglement (79% fatal) and ingestion harm. Effects include digestive blockage, reduced nutrition, respiration impairment, and potential chemical toxicity. Global presence includes oceans, soil, and atmosphere.

### Unit 3: Concept of Global Warming and Climate

#### 4.3.1–4.3.2 Global Warming and Implications

**Theory**

Global warming is the gradual increase in Earth's surface temperature due to rising greenhouse gases (CO₂, CO) trapping reflected sunlight. Essential in normal proportions for life, excess GHGs cause damaging heat accumulation — the greenhouse effect. IPCC (1998): world already 0.5°C warmer than pre-industrial period. Implications include desertification, droughts, reduced agriculture, polar ice melt, sea level rise, and climate refugees.

**Important Points**

- Global warming = central environmental issue; earth surface getting progressively warmer
- Sunlight absorbed by GHGs (CO₂) normally warms earth; excess GHGs trap more heat
- Also called greenhouse effect (similar to greenhouse heat trapping)
- Implications: fertile zones → deserts; droughts; reduced agriculture; polar melt → sea level rise
- 5% sea level rise displaces millions in low-tide areas; small island nations may disappear
- **Climate refugees** = people displaced by drought, floods, starvation — most visible symbol of global warming

**Causes of Climate Change (SLM — PYQ Q84)**

- **1. Rising greenhouse gases:** Increasing CO₂, CO, CH₄, N₂O and other GHGs from human activities
- **2. Fossil fuel burning:** Coal, oil, natural gas combustion since Industrial Revolution (+25% CO₂ since 1750)
- **3. Deforestation:** Removes carbon sinks; releases stored CO₂ into atmosphere
- **4. Industrial & transport emissions:** Factories, power plants, vehicles emit CO₂ and pollutants
- **5. Agriculture & livestock:** Methane (CH₄) from livestock and rice paddies; nitrous oxide (N₂O) from fertilisers
- **6. Greenhouse effect mechanism:** Excess GHGs absorb reflected sunlight → heat trapped → Earth's surface temperature rises gradually (global warming)

**Consequences of Climate Change (SLM — PYQ Q84)**

- **1. Desertification:** Fertile zones lose moisture → turn into dry lands and deserts
- **2. Droughts:** Many countries suffer prolonged water shortages
- **3. Reduced agriculture:** Crop yields fall; heat waves, floods, pests; lower protein/nutrients in crops
- **4. Ongoing temperature rise:** IPCC (1998) — world already 0.5°C warmer than pre-industrial period
- **5. Polar ice melt & sea-level rise:** Melting polar regions; even 5% sea-level rise displaces millions; small island nations may disappear
- **6. Climate refugees:** People displaced by drought, floods, starvation — most visible symbol of global warming
- **7. Sector impacts:** Livestock heat stress; fisheries species migration; forest wildfires/drought; water quality degradation and saltwater intrusion

#### 4.3.3–4.3.4 Greenhouse Gases, Greenhouse Effect, and International Agreements

**Theory**

Major GHG drivers include water vapour (most abundant, positive feedback loop), CO₂ (primary human-emitted, from fossil fuel combustion since Industrial Revolution ~1750), methane (25× more potent than CO₂ over 100 years), nitrous oxide (300× CO₂ warming impact), and fluorocarbons (HFCs, PFCs, SF₆, NF₃ — most potent, no natural sources). John Tyndall (1861) proposed CO₂ concentration raises surface temperature. International agreements — **Kyoto Protocol (1997/2005)** and **Paris Agreement (2015)** — aim to reduce global GHG emissions.

**Important Points**

**Complete GHG List**

- **Water vapour:** H₂O; Evaporation (feedback, not direct emission); Days; Most abundant; positive feedback loop
- **Carbon dioxide:** CO₂; Fossil fuels, deforestation, vehicles, industry; 50–200 years; Baseline (1×); +25% since Industrial Revolution
- **Methane:** CH₄; Livestock, natural gas leaks, wetlands, rice paddies; ~12 years; 25× CO₂ over 100 years
- **Nitrous oxide:** N₂O; Agriculture, fuel combustion, wastewater; 114 years; 300× CO₂; 40% from human activities
- **HFCs:** Various; Refrigerants, air conditioning; Years to centuries; Up to 12,000× CO₂
- **PFCs:** Various; Aluminium/semiconductor manufacturing; Thousands of years; Up to 10,000× CO₂
- **SF₆:** SF₆; Electrical equipment; 3,200 years; 23,900× CO₂
- **NF₃:** NF₃; Electronics manufacturing; 740 years; 17,200× CO₂
- **CFCs:** CFCs; Aerosols, refrigerants (now regulated); 50–100 years; Ozone-depleting + GHG

**International Climate Agreements**

- **UNFCCC:** 1992 (Rio Earth Summit) — United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change; foundation for climate negotiations
- **Kyoto Protocol:** Adopted 1997; effective 2005 — Legally binding emission reduction targets for developed nations; origin of carbon trading; Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) for developing countries; Marrakesh Accords formalised carbon credit market; divided countries into industrialised (emission trading) and developing (CDM)
- **Paris Agreement:** 2015 (COP21) — All nations set voluntary emission reduction targets (NDCs — Nationally Determined Contributions); aim to limit warming to well below 2°C, pursue 1.5°C; global stocktake every 5 years; no legally binding targets (unlike Kyoto)
- **Montreal Protocol:** 1987 — Phase out ozone-depleting substances (CFCs, halons); indirect climate benefit

- GHG concentrations measured in ppm, ppb, ppt
- Greenhouse effect: CO₂, CFCs, N₂O, CH₄ form heat-trapping cloud over earth
- UNFCCC = United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change

**For Exam**

Major greenhouse gases: water vapour (most abundant, feedback loop), CO₂ (primary human emission, +25% since 1750), methane (25× CO₂ potency), nitrous oxide (300× CO₂), and fluorocarbons (highest GWP, human-only). Kyoto Protocol (1997/2005) set binding emission targets for developed nations and created carbon trading/CDM. Paris Agreement (2015) requires all nations to submit NDCs aiming to limit warming below 2°C. UNFCCC (1992) is the foundation convention. #### 4.3.8–4.3.11 Management, Acid Rain, and Ozone Depletion

- **3.** Industrialised nations assist developing countries in limiting emissions
- **4.** All countries implement national strategies to limit toxic emissions
- **5.** Recognise climatic changes in economic, social, and environmental policies
- **6.** Increase public awareness through education and training on global warming
- **7.** Continuous international research cooperation on climate change
- **8.** Develop global environmental ethics — share resources equitably, not just consume

### Unit 4: Concept and Definition of Carbon Usage

#### 4.4.1 Carbon Sequestration

**Theory**

Carbon sequestration is long-term storage of carbon in plants, soils, geologic formations, and oceans to reduce atmospheric CO₂ and global climate change. It occurs naturally (photosynthesis) and artificially (capturing industrial CO₂ for underground storage). Types include biological (oceans, soils, forests, grasslands), geological, industrial (pre-combustion, post-combustion, oxyfuel), and technological (DAC, graphene, engineered molecules).

**Important Points**

- **Biological: Oceans:** Absorb ~25% of human CO₂ emissions — Colder, nutrient-rich polar regions are best carbon sinks; positive/negative flux
- **Biological: Soils:** SOC via photosynthesis; carbonates in arid soil — Agricultural practices restore depleted SOC; caliche in desert soils
- **Biological: Forests:** Capture ~25% of global emissions — Carbon released on fires/deforestation; trees = key carbon sinks
- **Biological: Grasslands:** More reliable than forests under climate stress — Carbon stays in roots/soil during burns; less vulnerable to wildfire
- **Geological:** Store CO₂ in underground rock formations — From industrial sources (steel, cement, power plants); injected into porous rocks
- **Industrial: Pre-combustion:** Remove carbon from fuel before burning — Coal reacted with oxygen → synthesis gas (CO + H₂)
- **Industrial: Post-combustion:** Scrub CO₂ from flue gases after burning — Waste gases captured and cleaned at smokestacks
- **Industrial: Oxyfuel:** Burn fuel in pure oxygen; capture all gases — All combustion products stored
- **Technological: DAC:** Direct Air Capture from atmosphere — Energy-intensive and costly at mass scale
- **Technological: Engineered molecules:** Molecules shaped to capture CO₂ from air — Filter approach targeting CO₂ specifically

**Advantages of Carbon Sequestration**

- Promotes tree planting and forest preservation (forests capture 25% of emissions)
- Reduces global warming (45% of CO₂ stays in atmosphere; sequestration removes it)
- Reduces ocean acidification (~30% of CO₂ absorbed by oceans raises acidity)
- Industrial carbon capture from coal plants and smokestacks

**Disadvantages of Carbon Sequestration**

- Reservoir leaks via rock fissures and tectonic processes may release stored CO₂
- Adds 1–5 cents/kWh to energy cost; nearly doubles modern coal technology cost
- DAC and technological methods too costly for mass-scale implementation

- **PYQ Q85 For Exam:** **Carbon sequestration** stores CO₂ to reduce atmospheric levels. **Ways:** **(1) Biological** — oceans (~25%), forests (~25%), soil/grassland; **(2) Geological** — inject CO₂ underground; **(3) Industrial pre-combustion** — capture before burning; **(4) Post-combustion/oxyfuel** — scrub flue gas. Advantages: reduces warming, reforestation; disadvantages: leak risk, increased cost.

**For Exam**

Carbon sequestration stores carbon in plants, soils, oceans, and geological formations to reduce atmospheric CO₂. #### 4.4.2 Carbon Footprint and Reduction Methods

**Theory**

Carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gases — primarily carbon dioxide — released into the atmosphere by a particular human activity, measured in tonnes of CO₂ per year. According to WHO, it measures the impact of activities on CO₂ produced through burning fossil fuels. It includes direct emissions (transport, heating) and indirect emissions (electricity, goods consumed, food production).

**Important Points**

**Carbon Footprint Calculator Factors**

- Home electricity use (depends on fuel type of power plant and consumption amount)
- Furnace/boiler emissions (efficiency, house size, insulation, fuel type)
- Vehicle use (fuel efficiency, distance driven, idling in traffic)
- Recycling habits (reducing waste sent to landfills reduces GHG from raw material processing)

**Methods of Reducing Carbon Footprint**

- **Drive efficient vehicles:** Lower fuel consumption = less CO₂ per km
- **Maintain vehicles properly:** Well-tuned engines burn fuel more efficiently
- **Use public transportation:** Shared transport reduces per-person emissions
- **Use energy-efficient appliances:** Less electricity consumed = less power plant emissions
- **Insulate home:** Reduces heating and air conditioning energy needs
- **Switch to LED lighting:** Transition from incandescent bulbs saves energy
- **Eat less meat:** Meat production has higher carbon footprint than fruits/vegetables
- **Buy local food:** Less transportation = less food-mile emissions
- **Recycle and reuse:** Reduces manufacturing emissions from raw material extraction
- **Purchase low-carbon products:** Manufacturers publishing carbon footprints enable informed choice
- **Buy carbon offsets:** Fund tree planting or renewable energy projects
- **Use farmer's market on foot:** Zero transport emissions for food purchase

- Carbon footprint includes methane, N₂O, CFCs — expressed as CO₂ equivalent (tonnes)
- Offsetting: purchase carbon credits funding tree planting or renewable energy projects

**Carbon Credits and Trading**

Carbon footprint is total greenhouse gases (primarily CO₂) released by an activity, individual, organization, or nation — measured in tonnes CO₂/year. Carbon credits are tradable permits allowing emission of one tonne CO₂ equivalent. Carbon trading (originated in Kyoto Protocol, 2005) buys/sells these credits to reduce overall emissions. Cap-and-trade sets emission caps declining annually; companies with surplus sell to those exceeding limits.

**Important Points**

- **Carbon footprint:** GHGs released by human activity (tonnes CO₂/year) — Direct emissions (transport, heating) + indirect (electricity, goods consumed); reduce via efficient vehicles, public transport, insulation, less meat, recycling
- **Carbon credit:** Tradable permit to emit 1 tonne CO₂ equivalent — VER (voluntary market) vs CER (certified, regulatory); formalized in Kyoto Protocol/Marrakesh Accords
- **Carbon trading:** Buying/selling emission credits — Kyoto Protocol (2005); ERPA contract; CDM for developing countries (CER credits)
- **Cap and trade:** Government sets emission cap; declining annually — Surplus sellers help over-emitters comply; aggregate pollution below baseline
- **Carbon offset:** Purchase credits funding tree planting/renewable energy — Individuals and companies offset unavoidable emissions

**Carbon Credit Trading Example (Cap-and-Trade)**

- **1. Government allocation** — Receives 1000 credits (cap). Receives 1000 credits (cap)
- **2. Actual emissions** — Emits only 700 tonnes CO₂. Emits 1200 tonnes CO₂
- **3. Credit status** — **Surplus. 300 credits**: **Deficit: 200 credits** (exceeds cap)
- **4. Options for B** — —. Pay government fine OR buy credits
- **5. Trade agreement** — Sells 200 surplus credits to B. Buys 200 credits from A
- **6. Outcome** — Earns money + positive image. Avoids fine; meets compliance
- **7. Net effect** — Total emissions capped at 2000 tonnes. Market incentivises emission reduction

- 2016 global CO₂ emissions ~36 billion metric tonnes
- Kyoto Protocol divided countries: industrialised (emission trading market) vs developing (CDM/CER credits)
- ERPA (Emission Reduction Purchase Agreement) = legal contract for carbon credit trading
- China started national emissions-trading programme (July 2021)

**For Exam**

Carbon footprint measures GHGs released (tonnes CO₂/year). Carbon credits are tradable permits for one tonne CO₂, created under Kyoto Protocol. In cap-and-trade: if Company A emits less than its cap (surplus credits), it sells to Company B which exceeds its cap — B avoids fines, A earns revenue, and total emissions stay within the national cap. Credit types: VER (voluntary) and CER (certified via CDM). Carbon trading incentivises emission reduction through market mechanisms.

#### 4.4.6–4.4.10 Environmental Economics, Green Economy, and Circular Economy

**Theory**

Environmental economics applies economic tools to allocate environmental resources efficiently, addressing market failure, externalities, and sustainable development. Originated in 1960s industrialisation concerns; led to UNEP (1972). Green economy harmonizes human-nature interaction with full-cost accounting. Circular economy extends product life through sharing, leasing, reusing, repairing, and recycling — designing out waste with renewable energy and non-toxic materials.

**Important Points**

**Five Concepts of Environmental Economics**

- **Sustainable development:** Meets present needs without compromising future generations — Four components: economic growth, environmental protection, social equity, institutional capacity
- **Market failure:** Perfect market cannot efficiently allocate environmental resources — Clean oceans — no market to price degree of cleanliness
- **Externalities:** Unintended consequences of economic activity affecting third parties — Negative: industrial pollution harms community; Positive: community park benefits visitors (free riders)
- **Valuation:** Assigning economic value to environmental resources — Use method (willingness to pay); non-use method (contingent valuation)
- **Cost-benefit analysis (CBA):** Weighing policy benefits against costs with discount factor — Best policy = greatest surplus of benefits over costs

- **Environmental economics:** Allocates environmental resources efficiently; prescriptive or incentive-based approaches; 3Es: Energy, Economy, Environment
- **Green economy:** Harmonious human-nature interaction; natural capital has economic value; full-cost accounting; polluters held liable (Blueprint for Green Economy, UK 1989); low carbon, resource efficient, socially inclusive
- **Circular economy:** Share, lease, reuse, repair, refurbish, recycle; design out waste; consumables = biological nutrients; durables = technical nutrients for reuse; C2C = Cradle to Cradle
- **Circular principles:** (1) Design out waste — products optimised for disassembly; (2) Separate consumables (biological) from durables (technical); (3) Renewable energy fuels the cycle

- Environmental economics challenges: transnational nature of pollution; impact on multiple society sectors
- Green economists advocate full cost accounting — entities harming natural assets held liable
- Circular economy replaces "end-of-life" concept with restoration; eliminates toxic chemicals

- **PYQ Q123 For Exam:** **Environmental economics** applies economic tools to environmental problems: **market failure**, **externalities** (pollution = negative externality), **valuation**, **cost-benefit analysis**. **Green economy** — full-cost accounting, polluters liable. **Circular economy** — share, reuse, repair, recycle. **3Es:** Energy, Economy, Environment. Sustainable resource allocation for current and future generations.

**For Exam**

Environmental economics (1960s origin, UNEP 1972) applies economic analysis to environmental resource allocation, addressing market failure (no market for clean oceans), externalities (pollution as negative externality), valuation, and cost-benefit analysis. Green economy harmonizes human-nature needs with full-cost accounting where polluters are liable. Circular economy minimizes waste through sharing, reusing, repairing, and recycling — three principles: design out waste, separate biological/technical nutrients, use renewable energy. 3Es: Energy, Economy, Environment.

#### 4.4.11–4.4.12 Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA)

**Theory**

EIA evaluates likely environmental impacts of proposed projects before decision-making, considering socio-economic, cultural, and health impacts. UNEP defines EIA as a tool identifying environmental, social, and economic impacts prior to decisions. It predicts effects early, proposes mitigation, and enables environmentally sound project design. The EIA process is cyclical with interaction between various steps.

**Important Points**

**EIA Process Steps (Complete Table)**

- **1:** **Screening**; Project plan screened for scale of investment, location, type of development; determine if statutory clearance needed; Decision: EIA required or exempt
- **2:** **Scoping**; Identify potential impacts, zone of impacts, mitigation possibilities, monitoring needs; Terms of Reference (TOR) for EIA study
- **3:** **Baseline data collection**; Document environmental status of study area — air, water, soil, biodiversity, noise, socio-economic conditions; Baseline environmental report
- **4:** **Impact prediction**; Predict positive/negative, reversible/irreversible, temporary/permanent impacts on all environmental components; Impact prediction matrix
- **5:** **Mitigation measures**; Propose prevention, minimisation, or bypassing of adverse impacts; compensation for environmental damage; Mitigation plan
- **6:** **EIA report preparation**; Compile all findings into formal Environmental Impact Assessment report; Draft EIA report
- **7:** **Public hearing**; Inform and consult public and environmental groups living near project site; collect objections/suggestions; Public hearing proceedings
- **8:** **Decision making**; Impact Assessment Authority reviews EIA + EMP (Environment Management Plan) with experts and project proponent; Grant or reject environmental clearance
- **9:** **Monitoring & EMP implementation**; Monitor compliance during all project phases — construction, operations, decommissioning; Compliance reports
- **10:** **Assessment of alternatives**; Compare project location and process technology alternatives; select least-impact option; Alternative analysis
- **11:** **Risk assessment**; Inventory analysis; hazard probability and index for catastrophic risks; Risk assessment report

**EIA Stakeholders**

- Project proponent (applicant)
- Environmental consultant (prepares EIA on behalf of proponent)
- Pollution Control Board — CPCB (central) / SPCB (state)
- Public (right to express opinion at hearing)
- Impact Assessment Authority
- MoEFCC Regional Centre

**EIA Benefits:** cost-effective impact elimination; informed decision-making; community participation; resource optimization; protection of environment; saves time and cost

**EIA Shortcomings:** exempt projects; inexperienced expert committees; inadequate public hearing; fraudulent data; confidential EMPs for strategic industries (nuclear)

- **PYQ Q86 For Exam:** **Importance of EIA:** evaluates environmental, social and economic impacts **before** project approval — informed decisions, cost-effective impact prevention, **community participation**, resource optimization, sustainable development. Identifies adverse effects early for mitigation. Stakeholders: proponent, consultant, CPCB/SPCB, public, MoEFCC. Prevents irreversible environmental damage from unchecked development.

- **PYQ Q63 For Exam:** **Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA)** evaluates likely **environmental, social and economic impacts** of proposed projects **before** decision-making. **Aim:** enable informed decisions, identify adverse effects early, incorporate **mitigation measures**, ensure **community participation**, optimize resources and promote **sustainable development**. UNEP defines EIA as a tool for identifying impacts prior to decisions.

### Unit 5: A Brief Overview of Prominent Natural Disasters in India

#### 4.5.1–4.5.3 Landslides

**Theory**

A landslide is mass movement of rock, debris, or earth down a slope under gravity (mass wasting). Caused by slope oversteepening, water saturation, earthquakes, volcanic activity, weathering, and human activity (mining, deforestation). Types by movement: slides, flows, spreads, topples, falls. Mitigation includes restricting populations in hazard zones, early warning systems, slope modification, retaining walls, and drainage diversion.

**Important Points**

**Causes and Susceptibility Factors**

- **Heavy rainfall:** Saturates slope material; reduces friction and cohesion; adds weight
- **Erosion:** Undercuts slope base (river, sea, excavation); slope becomes too steep
- **Earthquakes:** Short-term stress triggers slope failure; common in hilly regions
- **Volcanic activity:** Seismic shaking + debris accumulation on slopes
- **Deforestation:** Removes vegetation binding soil; increases surface runoff
- **Mining/traffic vibrations:** Disturbs slope stability; alters drainage patterns
- **Weathering:** Freeze-thaw reduces cohesion between rock grains
- **Slope angle:** Steeper slopes more susceptible; coastal erosion increases angle
- **Rock type:** Sand: high friction, low cohesion; Clay: high cohesion, low friction
- **Water content:** Weakens sandy materials (reduces interparticle friction); dissolves clay cements

**Types of Landslides**

| Type | Movement | Material / Feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Slides** | Along discrete shearing surface | Translational or rotational/slump |
| **Flows** | Viscous fluid-like movement | Debris, earth, mud; water fluidizes |
| **Spreads** | Lateral movement on weak substrate | Liquefaction; riverbank retrogression |
| **Topples** | Outward rotation of rock mass | Rotation from steep slope face |
| **Falls** | Free fall of rock/debris | Detachment from steep cliff or slope |

**Mitigation and Prevention**

- Hazard zoning: restrict populations in landslide-prone areas
- Early warning: monitor strain in rocks/soils, slope displacement, groundwater levels
- Engineering: retaining walls, piles, grouting rock joints, debris pathway diversion
- Drainage: reroute surface and underwater drainage away from unstable slopes
- Slope geometry modification; chemical agents to reinforce slope material

**For Exam**

A landslide is gravity-driven mass movement of rock, debris, or earth down a slope. Causes include heavy rainfall (water saturation), erosion, earthquakes, deforestation, and mining. Five types: slides (translational/rotational), flows (viscous, water-fluidized), spreads (lateral on liquefied substrate), topples (outward rotation), and falls. Water weakens sandy slopes (reduces friction) and clay slopes (dissolves cements). Mitigation uses hazard zoning, early warning systems, retaining walls, grouting, and drainage management.

#### 4.5.4 Floods

**Theory**

A flood is water overflow submerging normally dry land — most common weather-related natural disaster. Caused by massive rainfall, river overflow, dam collapse, snowmelt, deforestation, climate change, and greenhouse gas emissions. Five types: river flood, coastal flood, storm surge, inland flood, and flash flood.

**Important Points**

**Causes of Floods (SLM — PYQ Q101)**

| # | Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Heavy rainfall** | Drainage systems overwhelmed; excess water cannot be stored |
| **2** | **River overflow** | Rivers exceed bank capacity; dams mismanaged |
| **3** | **Dam/levee collapse** | Structural failure releases stored water suddenly |
| **4** | **Snow/glacier melt** | Rapid melting increases river water volume |
| **5** | **Deforestation** | Loss of trees increases surface runoff and erosion |
| **6** | **Climate change** | Altered rainfall patterns; more extreme weather events |
| **7** | **Urbanisation** | Paved surfaces and poor drainage worsen inland flooding |
| **8** | **Coastal/storm surge** | Abnormal sea-level rise during cyclones and high tides |

**Five Types of Flooding**

| Type | Description | Cause / Example |
|---|---|---|
| **River flood** | Water exceeds river banks | Tropical storms, snowmelt (Kerala 2018) |
| **Coastal flood** | Land submerged by seawater | Tidal surges, high winds, tsunamis |
| **Storm surge** | Abnormal coastal water rise above tide | Wind + waves + low pressure |
| **Inland flood** | Not coastal; rainfall/snowmelt flooding | Worse in urban areas with poor drainage |
| **Flash flood** | Begins within 3–6 hours of heavy rain | Dam/levee failure, ice jam release |

- Chennai floods (2016), Kerala floods (2018, 2019) are prominent Indian examples
- Flood size measured by peak water level ('flood peak')
- Environmental consequences: weed dispersal, soil erosion, pollutant release, habitat destruction
- Flash floods most dangerous due to sudden onset with little warning

- **PYQ Q101 For Exam:** The main **causes of floods** include **heavy rainfall**, **river overflow**, **dam or levee collapse**, **snow/glacier melt**, **deforestation** (increases surface runoff), **climate change** (extreme weather), **urbanisation** (paved surfaces and poor drainage), and **storm surges** along coasts. Deforestation and climate change increase flood risk. Indian examples: Chennai 2016, Kerala 2018–19.

#### 4.5.5–4.5.7 Cyclones and Earthquakes

**Theory**

Cyclones are atmospheric disturbances around low-pressure areas with destructive circular winds. Tropical cyclones (10°–25° N/S) are called hurricanes/typhoons in northern hemisphere (anti-clockwise); cyclones in southern hemisphere (clockwise). Extratropical cyclones form in mid-latitudes at frontal zones. Earthquakes are sudden ground shaking from seismic waves when rock masses fracture and slip along fault planes — recorded by seismographs; located by triangulation using three seismographs.

**Important Points**

**Cyclones**

- **Tropical cyclone:** Forms over warm tropical oceans (10°–25° N/S); winds >119 km/h (74 mph); energy from warm sea surface; organised surface wind circulation — Bulbul struck West Bengal (2019); rotates clockwise in Southern Hemisphere, anti-clockwise in Northern Hemisphere
- **Extratropical cyclone:** Mid/high latitude frontal zones; wave cyclones; less violent than tropical; temperature variations drive formation — Common globally at mid-latitudes
- **Eye of cyclone:** Calm, cloudless centre; no rain; very light winds; low pressure area — Most distinctive feature of cyclone structure
- **Storm components:** Wind + waves + low atmospheric pressure create storm surge — Can exceed 20 feet when combined with high tide

**Earthquakes**

- **Hypocentre (Focus):** Location below earth's surface where earthquake starts — Point of initial rock fracture
- **Epicentre:** Location directly above hypocentre on earth's surface — Area of maximum surface damage; located by triangulation
- **Fault / Fault plane:** Fracture surface where rock masses slip past each other — Normal fault: hanging wall moves down relative to footwall
- **P waves (Primary):** Longitudinal body waves; fastest; travel through solids and liquids — First waves to hit seismographs; direction of motion = direction of propagation
- **S waves (Secondary/Shear):** Transverse body waves; slower than P waves; travel through solids only — Motion perpendicular to propagation; used to map earth's interior
- **Surface waves:** Travel on earth's surface; cause maximum destruction — Most damaging to buildings and infrastructure
- **Seismograph:** Instrument recording earthquake waves — Base fixed to ground; heavy weight hangs free on spring; records as seismogram
- **Triangulation:** Method using 3 seismographs to locate epicentre — Requires three stations at different locations

- Cyclones classified: (i) tropical cyclones, (ii) extratropical cyclones
- Earthquakes most frequent in Pacific Ocean
- Energy stored at stuck fault edges released as seismic waves when friction overcome
- Bulbul = severe cyclonic storm, West Bengal 2019; hurricanes = Atlantic tropical cyclones

**For Exam**

Cyclones are revolving tropical storms around low-pressure centres. Tropical cyclones form over warm oceans (10°–25°) with winds exceeding 119 km/h; the calm "eye" has no rain. Bulbul struck West Bengal in 2019. Extratropical cyclones occur at mid-latitude frontal zones. Earthquakes: hypocentre (underground origin), epicentre (surface point above), fault (slip plane). P waves (fastest, longitudinal, solids+liquids), S waves (transverse, solids only), surface waves (maximum destruction). Seismographs record earthquakes; triangulation with 3 stations locates epicentre. Earthquakes most frequent in Pacific Ocean.

### Unit 6: Important Acts and Rules for the Conservation of Environment

#### 4.6.1–4.6.4 Wildlife Protection Act, 1972

**Theory**

The Wildlife Protection Act, 1972 protects wild animals, birds, and plant species for environmental and ecological security, restricting hunting and establishing protected area networks. Constitutional basis: Article 48A (protect environment, wildlife, forests — 42nd Amendment 1976) and Article 51A(g) (fundamental duty to protect environment). History: Wild Birds Protection Act 1887 → 1912 → 1935 amendment; serious protection from 1960s. India became CITES party; established National Board for Wildlife and National Tiger Conservation Authority.

**Important Points**

**Salient Features of Wildlife Protection Act, 1972**

- Protects listed species of animals, birds, and plants; ensures ecological security
- **Six schedules** giving varying protection degrees:
  - Schedule I and Schedule II (Part II) = **absolute protection**, maximum penalties
  - Schedules III–VI = decreasing protection levels; some species may be hunted under licence
- Prohibits hunting of endangered species; regulates trade of scheduled animals
- Establishes wildlife sanctuaries, national parks, conservation reserves, community reserves, tiger reserves
- Provides licences for sale, transfer, and possession of some wildlife species
- India became party to **CITES** (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species / Washington Convention)
- First comprehensive list of endangered wildlife of India prepared under this Act

**Key Bodies Established**

- **National Board for Wildlife:** Under WPA 1972 — Statutory advisory body; chaired by Prime Minister; approves national park/sanctuary projects; promotes conservation
- **National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA):** Under WPA 1972 — Statutory body under MoEFCC; strengthens Project Tiger (1973); protects tigers from extinction
- **Central Zoo Authority:** 1992 — Oversees zoos in India; ensures proper animal care and breeding programmes

**Five Protected Area Types**

- **1. Sanctuaries** — limited human activity; refuge for injured/abused wildlife
- **2. National Parks** — no human activity; fixed boundaries; Bandipur, Karnataka
- **3. Conservation Reserves** — state-declared adjacent to sanctuaries/parks
- **4. Community Reserves** — private/community land for wildlife conservation
- **5. Tiger Reserves** — for tiger protection on NTCA recommendation

**For Exam**

Wildlife Protection Act, 1972 protects wild animals, birds, and plants, restricting hunting and establishing protected areas. Constitutional support: Article 48A and Article 51A(g). Six schedules provide graded protection — Schedule I gets absolute protection with maximum penalties. India joined CITES (Washington Convention). National Board for Wildlife (PM-chaired) advises on conservation; NTCA oversees Project Tiger (1973); Central Zoo Authority (1992) oversees zoos. Five protected area types: sanctuaries, national parks, conservation reserves, community reserves, and tiger reserves.

#### 4.6.5–4.6.7 Key Environmental Acts — Provisions Comparison

**Theory**

India's four major environmental acts form an interconnected legal framework: Wildlife Protection Act (1972) for biodiversity, Water Act (1974) for water pollution, Air Act (1981) for air pollution, and Environment Protection Act (1986) as umbrella legislation coordinating all environmental laws. CPCB (central) and SPCBs (state) enforce pollution control under Air and Water Acts.

**Important Points**

**Key Environmental Acts — Comparison**

| Act | Year | Purpose | Key Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wildlife Protection Act** | 1972 (amended 2006) | Protect wild animals, birds, plants | 6 schedules; sanctuaries, NPs, NTCA; Article 48A, 51A(g) |
| **Water Pollution Act** | 1974 | Prevent and control water pollution | CPCB + SPCB; Water Cess Act 1977 |
| **Air Pollution Act** | 1981 | Prevent and control air pollution | CPCB + SPCB; noise added 1987 |
| **Environment Protection Act** | 1986 | Umbrella environmental framework | Coordinates Water/Air Acts; up to 5–7 years imprisonment |

**Sanctuary vs National Park (Wildlife Act)**

| Feature | Wildlife Sanctuary | National Park |
|---|---|---|
| **Human activity** | Limited (timber, minor forest products) | No human activity allowed |
| **Boundaries** | Not generally fixed | Fixed and defined |
| **Grazing/livestock** | Permitted with restrictions | Not permitted |
| **Declaration** | State government notification | State government notification |
| **Upgrade/downgrade** | Can upgrade to National Park | Cannot downgrade to sanctuary |
| **Example** | Refuge for injured/abused wildlife | Bandipur National Park, Karnataka |

- Wildlife Act history: Wild Birds Protection Act 1887 → 1912 → 1935 amendment → serious protection from 1960s
- Environment Act = first concrete step for air pollution (Air Act) + umbrella for all environmental coordination
- Water Cess Act 1977 augments CPCB and SPCB resources

- **PYQ Q87 For Exam:** **Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981** — prevents, controls and abates air pollution. Defines **air pollutant** (including **noise**, 1987 amendment). Establishes **CPCB** (central) and **SPCB** (state) with powers to inspect, sample, regulate emissions, set standards and penalize violations. Works with Environment Protection Act 1986 for comprehensive pollution control in India.

**For Exam**

India's four key environmental acts: (1) Wildlife Protection Act (1972) — protects species via 6 schedules, establishes sanctuaries/national parks, NTCA, National Board for Wildlife; (2) Water Act (1974) — prevents water pollution via CPCB/SPCB, prohibits excess discharge; (3) Air Act (1981) — prevents air pollution, defines pollutants including noise (1987), establishes CPCB/SPCB; (4) Environment Protection Act (1986) — umbrella legislation coordinating all acts, sets emission standards, hazardous waste rules, penalties up to 5–7 years imprisonment.
